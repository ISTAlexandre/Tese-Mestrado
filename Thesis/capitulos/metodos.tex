\chapter{Data and analysis}
\label{cap:metodos}

The preceding chapter established the observables through which collinear spin
correlations can be accessed. Following that, this chapter describes how they are
measured in practice and presents the data on which this analysis is built, the
software environment in which it runs, and the sequence of steps that leads from the
raw available events to the azimuthal distributions of Chapter \ref{cap:resultados}.
The goal of this chapter is to reveal all the steps necessary to make this analysis,
securing reproducibility. That said, enough detail is given, and the code used is
available publicly in \cite{Mendonca:CMSJetSubstructure}, 
so that the analysis can be repeated in full from public inputs alone.

The chapter is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:opendata} introduces the
CERN Open Data initiative and the principles that make an analysis of this kind
possible outside the experimental collaborations. Section~\ref{sec:cms} describes
the relevant features of the CMS detector, and Section~\ref{sec:dataset} the data
format and the specific dataset used. Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} presents the
analysis pipeline, from the extraction of jet constituents to the computation of
the spin observables. Section~\ref{sec:flavour} describes the flavour selection
that isolates the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel, and Section~\ref{sec:systematic}
assesses the systematic uncertainties affecting the measurement.

\section{CERN Open Data}
\label{sec:opendata}

The data analysed in this work were obtained through the CERN Open Data
Portal~\cite{CERNOpenData}, the platform through which the LHC experiments
release reconstructed collision data and the associated simulation to the public.
Launched in 2014, the portal was the first initiative of its kind in high-energy
physics, and it has since grown into a major repository holding data from
the ALICE, ATLAS, CMS and LHCb collaborations. The first releases came from the
CMS experiment, and it is CMS data that this work uses.

The CERN Open Data Portal does not provide detector output but data at the same level
of reconstruction used in collaborations' own published analysis. These datasets
include physics objects such as jets, tracks and reconstructed particles, with the
calibration already applied and run quality information required to use them correctly.
Alongside the collision data, the portal also releases the corresponding Monte Carlo simulations,
the software environment necessary to analyse the datasets and documentation describing their
format and content. All this features of the portal make it possible for any user to
use their data for their own analysis and interpret the results.

The importance of this portal in this work is twofold. First, as made clear in this
chapter's introduction, it makes the measurement reproducible. Every input from 
the collision and respective simulated datasets are publicly available, so 
that the analysis can be repeated in full by anyone. Second, the portal specifies,
for each dataset, the steps required to use them correctly. For each dataset used 
in this work, CMS provides a list of available and validated runs, with the 
proper calibrations and standard energy corrections and residual effects, applied by them.

This type of analysis are feasible outside the collaborations has been demonstrated
by a growing body of work built on CMS Open Data. Studies using released CMS
samples have addressed problems ranging from the discrimination of quark and
gluon initiated jets~\cite{Komiske_2022} to the measurement of $N$-point energy
correlators inside jets~\cite{Komiske_2023}, the latter closely related to the
observables of the present work. These analyses establish both that public data
retain the information needed for substructure measurements and that results of
genuine physics interest can be extracted from them. The present work is situated
in this line. It applies the same public data to the measurement of collinear spin
correlations, an effect not previously accessed in this way.

The only small downside of working with public data is the choice of software
environment. The released CMS data are stored in formats tied to the experiment's
reconstruction software, and reading them requires a compatible environment. 
CMS provides this in the form of a dedicated container image, inside docker \cite{docker2026}, that reproduces the
software stack in which the data were generated. In this work that environment was
used to read the released files and extract the jet and constituent information
required by the analysis, which was then carried out in a lighter, independent
environment. The details of this arrangement are given in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}.

\section{The CMS detector}
\label{sec:cms}

The collision data used and analysed in this work was recorded by the Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS).
This giant detector, alongside with ATLAS, is a general-purpose detector, at the LHC \cite{CMS:2008}.
It is a cylindrical apparatus with 21.6~m in length and 14.6~m of diameter, built in concentric
layers around the beam pipe with the characteristic feature of having a superconducting solenoid, with 
6~m in internal diameter, capable of producing a uniform 3.8~T magnetic field, that bends the trajectories
of charged particles allowing for their momenta to be measured. Its layers are capable of capturing
relevant features of this analysis and they will be described below in the order of a particle transversing them.

The CMS coordinate system has the origin centered at the nominal collision point inside the detector, its
y-axis points vertically upwards and the x-axis points radially inwards towards the center of the LHC.
This makes the z-axis point along the beam axis towards Jura mountains from LHC Point 5 \cite{CMS:2008}.
The polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the $z$ axis, The azimuthal angle $\phi$ is measured
from the x-axis in the x-y plane and the radial coordinate in this plane is denoted by $r$. The polar
angle $\theta$ is measured from the $z$ axis, although it is conventional to use the pseudorapidity
$\eta = -\ln\tan(\theta/2)$, because differences in $\eta$ are invariant under boosts along the beam.
The transverse momentum is denoted as $\pt$ and it is the component of momentum in the plane perpendicular
to the beam. These are the variables which jets and their constituents are used on this work.

Starting from the layer closest to the interaction point, the inner-tracker is an all-silicon
system of pixel and strip detectos covering $|\eta| < 2.5$. This layer is placed inside the solenoid's
field and it reconstructs the trajectories of charged particles, measures their momenta and detects 
the primary interaction vertex. Since substructure observables used in this work are built from 
the trajectories and momenta of a jet's charged constituents, this tracker is the subdetector which
these measurements directly rely on.

There are two calorimeters surrounding the inner-tracker. The electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL) uses
lead tungstate scintillating crystal to measure the energy of electrons and photons. Surrounding it,
the hardron calorimeter (HCAL), a brass/scintillator device, measures the energy of the hadrons. Together,
these two calorimeters cover the central region and, with more calorimeters forward extending the coverage
to $|\eta| < 5$, give CMS a near complete solid-angle coverage needed to measure the flow of energy in an event.
The muon system outside the solenoid, plays no important role for this analysis.

When CMS reconstructs jets, they don't rely on just one of these subdetectors isolated. Instead,
they use their own particle-flow algorithm, tuned specifically for the CMS detector \cite{CMS:2017}, which
combines signals from all the subdetectors, including tracks from the inner-tracker and energy deposits from
the calorimeters, to to reconstruct a list of individual particles in
each event, the particle-flow candidates. It is these candidates that are the inputs to the jets'
clustering algorithms of Section \ref{sec:ircsafety}, and their charged component measured by the tracker
that most directly determines the directions from which the substructure observables are built.
This particle-flow algorithm is therefore the point at
which the detector described here connects to the analysis that follows, and the
data format in which its output is released is the subject of the next section. The following
figure \ref{fig:cms}, gives a solid perspective of the CMS detector.

\label{sec:dataset}

for an analysis using their datasets, since it fixes what information is available and what variables
are at hand. The three formats relevant here are summarised in

For this work, the requirement is having access to the constituents of a jet. The substructure
observables of Chapter \ref{cap:estado-da-arte} are built by declustering a jet into its constituents,
so this analysis requires the individual particle-flow candidates that build the jet and not just 
a summary of the jet's kinematics. This immediality invalidates the NanoAOD data format since the particle-flow
candidates are not stored there. The AOD format stores the particle-flow candidates, however it also stores
other low-level reconstruction details which this analysis does not need making it occupy a large volume of
space. This leaves the MiniAOD data format, which occupies the middle ground of space and still
stores particle-flow candidates required for substructure while discarding the low-level information that is not
needed. Therefore, it is for this reason that the MiniAOD data format was the choice made for the format to be used
throughout this work.

Between all the MiniAOD datasets, this work analysed the JetHT primary dataset from the 2016 data-taking
period, in MiniAOD format~\cite{JetHT2016G}. Its characteristics are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:dataset}. The data are proton--proton collisions at a
centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s} = 13$~TeV, recorded during run period G of 2016
and released as part of the Ultra-Legacy reprocessing, the final and most complete
calibration of the Run~2 data.

The JetHT dataset is well suited for this work analysis since it is enriched in jet events. CMS records
events online that pass a two-level trigger system, composed by a hardware Level-1 trigger followed by a software High Level Trigger
(HLT) \cite{CMS:2017trigger}. The events of this dataset are then those accepted by a set of HLT paths,
which require either a large scalar sum of jet transverse momenta
or the presence of one or two energetic jets. As a result the sample is dominated
by the high-transverse-momentum, high-multiplicity final states in which jet
substructure is most informative. Since the dataset is served by triggers based on large-radius jets, events are
required to pass one of the \texttt{HLT\_AK8PFJet} paths. The analysis is
restricted to jets with transverse momentum above $700$~GeV, where the
unprescaled paths of highest threshold, \texttt{HLT\_AK8PFJet450} and
\texttt{HLT\_AK8PFJet500}, are almost fully efficient. In this region any jet passing
the selection also satisfies these paths, so the trigger requirement introduces
no turn-on bias, and the sample sits in the hard, perturbative regime
motivated in Section~\ref{sec:qcd}.

Furthermore, as required for all analysis of this dataset, the data is restricted to a list of
runs certified as good physics, provided with the dataset release. So, events not in these
certified runs do not enter this analysis. In addition, jets in this released sample do not 
require any energy calibrations to be applied to them, since they already carry the standard jet energy corrections applied by CMS
in the Ultra-Legacy reprocessing.

Alongside the JetHT collision dataset, this analysis uses a simulated sample of QCD
multijet events. This is because the flavour of a splitting inside a jet cannot be accessed in data,
so it is not possible to determine the secondary splitting channel. What the simulation
provides is the set of generated partons, carrying flavour, which are clustered in the
same way as the reconstructed particles in data. First the partons are clustered into jets with the
anti-$\kt$ algorithm of section \ref{sec:antikt} and then, through the Cambridge/Aachen algorithm of Section
\ref{sec:ca} and the IFN algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:ifn}, into a branching
history in which each splitting acquires a flavour. It is from this process that 
the channel of each splitting, $g\to q\bar{q}$, $g\to gg$ or $q\to qg$, is labelled. 
This flavour labelling process and how it is further used in collision data is discussed 
with more detail in Section \ref{sec:flavour}. 
The simulated samples are QCD multijet datasets generated with
\textsc{Pythia}~8~\cite{Sj_strand_2015} using the CP5
tune~\cite{CMS:2019csb}, passed through the full simulation of the CMS detector
and released in the same format as the collision data (MiniAODSIM). They are
produced in intervals of the partonic transverse momentum $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}}$.
The interval $800 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 1000$~GeV~\cite{QCDMC2016_800to1000}
is used to train the flavour selection, and the independent interval
$600 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 800$~GeV~\cite{QCDMC2016_600to800} to compute
its purity.

From these two datasets, there are some quantities extracted from each event, which are listed in
Table \ref{tab:variables}. From each jet in collision datasets, their constituents
are stored, since they are needed for the declustering of Chapter~\ref{cap:estado-da-arte} and
to build the Lund jet plane. The electric charge of
each constituent is extracted in addition to its kinematics, as it is used later in
the flavour selection of Section~\ref{sec:flavour}. In the simulated datasets, the
same jet and constituent quantities are extracted, together with the final-state
generated partons of the event, each with its identity. These partons carry the
flavour information from which the splitting flavour is determined, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:flavour}.

The jets themselves are taken from the \texttt{slimmedJetsAK8} collection, which are the
\antikt jets with $R=0.8$ stored in MiniAOD, in a way that the jet definition of
Section~\ref{sec:antikt} is applied by CMS, making it not necessary to repeat this step in this analysis.
Beyond the requirement of the transverse momentum described earlier in this section, jets
are required to lie within $|\eta| < 1.7$, keeping them in the
central, well-instrumented region of the detector. Events are further required to
contain at least two such jets separated in azimuth by more than $2$~radians,
selecting the back to back dijet topology in which the two hard jets recoil
against one another. In addition jet's constituents are required to have $\pt > 1 \text{GeV}$ in
order to be recorded along with the jet. This selection follows closely that of the CMS measurement on
which the present analysis is based~\cite{CMS:2026jnt}, applied here to the
earlier public dataset. The complete set of requirements is summarised in

\section{Analysis pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The analysis made in this work, proceeds in a sequence of steps that start from the 
collision and simulated datasets, described in Section \ref{sec:dataset}, and end
with the final azimuthal distributions. These steps are split into two parts,
separated by the software environment in which each one runs. The first part is the 
extraction of the jet's properties and constituent information from the datases. 
This step, as indicated by CMS, must be done inside  the CMS Open Data container or the CMS Virtual Machine
, since reading the MiniAOD format requires the experiment's own reconstruction software. In this
work, this step was done using the CMS Open Data docker container (\textsc{CMSSW}) with, for each dataset, the recommended
version of it. The second step covers declustering of the jets and the computation of the observables.
It is done in a separate environment built on ROOT \cite{Brun:1997pa}, \textsc{FastJet}~\cite{Cacciari_2012} and Python.
This separation makes the user able to use the \textsc{CMSSW} software just for a single step which is
completely mandatory and allows the rest of the analysis to run in a modern and
portable environment.

The stages of the pipeline are ilustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pipeline} and described in the following
subsections. Section~\ref{sec:extraction} describes
the extraction of the jet ntuples from the collision and simulated samples.
Section~\ref{sec:declustering-impl} describes the reclustering and declustering of
each jet and the construction of the Lund observables and of $\dpsi$.
Section~\ref{sec:genlevel} describes how the same procedure is applied to the
generated partons in simulation in order to label the channel of each splitting.
Section~\ref{sec:eeec-impl} describes the computation of the three-point energy
correlator. The outputs of these stages feed the flavour selection of
Section~\ref{sec:flavour} and the measurement presented in
Chapter~\ref{cap:resultados}. Since the datasets are extremely large, with a large
number of jets and its variables, every stage has parallelisation integration. For the
most demanding step of this analysis, which was the extraction of the variables, the code was 
run with the LIP's computing cluster, making it an huge difference for the time required to
complete this step. The code used is publicly available~\cite{Mendonca:CMSJetSubstructure}, so that each stage can be
reproduced from the released data alone.

\subsection{Extraction}
\label{sec:extraction}

The first stage of this work's pipeline is to convert the released MiniAOD and MiniAODSIM datasets
into compact ROOT ntuples with all the extracted information necessary for the rest of the analysis.
This stage runs inside the CMS open data container, where the collections present inside the datasets
are accessed through FWLite \cite{CMS:FWLite}, a lightweight Python interface to the CMS event data
model. The script developed by this work is capable of analysing datasets without downloading them.
In order to archieve that, the dataset's files are listed in the file indices provided with each
record on the CERN Open Data Portal and can be read remotelly from the portal's storage. This
completely avoids downloading/transferring the several terabytes of the full dataset but sacrifices
a little bit of the execution time of the script. Since there are multiple files and all different 
from each other, this script is parallelised as an array of jobs on a computing cluster. 
Each one of this jobs processes a portion of all the dataset's files, creating for each file
its own output file. After all the jobs are completed, a script was made to merge all the 
output files into a single one with all the extracted variables of the dataset.

For the collision data, each event goes through a sequence of requirements. As required by CMS,
each event is first checked against the list of certified luminosity sections, also provided 
with the datasets, and discarded if its run or luminosity sections are not certified for physics.
Then, the event is required to have triggered at least one of the \texttt{HLT\_AK8PFJet} trigger
paths. After passing these two conditions, the jets of the \texttt{slimmedJetsAK8} collection are read off
and those satisfying the transverse-momentum and pseudorapidity requirements of
Table~\ref{tab:selection} are extracted. For each extracted jet, its kinematics are
stored together with the list of its constituents, with $\pt > 1 \text{GeV}$, accompanied by their kinematics and electric charge.
Finally, the event is kept only if it contains at least two extracted jets separated in azimuth by more
than $2$~radians, in which case it is written to the output tree.

The simulated sample is processed in a similar way with two differences. The run and luminosity
certification and trigger requirements are not applied, since they concern real conditions of 
data taking and have no counterpart in simulation. Because the
selected jets lie in the region where the trigger is almost fully efficient,
omitting it in simulation does not introduce a difference between the two
samples. In addition, a reconstructed jet and its constituents are only extracted if it has an associated
generator-level jet, so that every simulated jet entering the analysis has a
counterpart at generator level. Furthermore, for each event, the final state partons 
are also stored. These partons are quarks and gluons of the generator level record
that do not split into other partons. In order words, they are the last partons of the shower
before hadronisation. These final state partons are stored for the event as a whole,
with their kinematics and identity, and are not
associated to any reconstructed jet. This step is only done at a later stage described in
Section \ref{sec:genlevel}.

The output of this stage is then a ROOT tree per sample, in which each entry
corresponds to an event and holds the selected jets, their constituents and, in
simulation, the generated partons, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:variables}. After adquiring
this tree, the CMS software is no longer needed for the remaining stages of this work's pipeline,
since it is not in a MiniAOD data format and instead a simple ROOT tree.

\subsection{Declustering and Lund observables}
\label{sec:declustering-impl}

The second stage of this pipeline picks up the output ROOT tree, from the previous stage, and
computes, for each jet present, the Lund declustering and the observables built from it. 
This script is written in C++ and uses ROOT and \textsc{FastJet} \cite{Cacciari_2012}, running
outside the CMS container. Each ntuple file can be processed independently, so the
stage is parallelised across files in the same way as the extraction, although it can also
be ran in a single merged output file. The variables computed by this script are stored
in the same tree per jet as new branches, so that a single file holds, for every jet, both the extracted information and the
observables derived from it.

For each event inside the ROOT tree, the script processes the jets in decreasing order of their
transverse momentum, and each jet contributes to the analysis. The stored constituents of the
jet are first converted into four-momenta from their transverse momentum, pseudorapidity, azimuth and mass, following equations \ref{eq:cartesian}-\ref{eq:energy},
and each is tagged with its electric charge so that it can be traced back after the clustering process.
The constituents are then reclustered with the C/A algorithm of Section \ref{sec:ca}, using 
$R=2$. This value is far larger than the angular size of an AK8 jet ($0.8$), since it
guarantees that all the constituents of a single \antikt jet are gathered into a single C/A
object and that no part of the jet is lost during the reclustering. 
Jets with fewer than two constituents cannot be
declustered and are flagged as failures.

\begin{equation}
  p_{x,i} = p_{\mathrm{T},i}\cos\phi_i, \qquad
  p_{y,i} = p_{\mathrm{T},i}\sin\phi_i, \qquad
  p_{z,i} = p_{\mathrm{T},i}\sinh\eta_i,
  \label{eq:cartesian}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
  E_i = \sqrt{p_{x,i}^2 + p_{y,i}^2 + p_{z,i}^2 + m_i^2} .
  \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}

After the reclustering, the full history is then declustered with the \textsc{LundGenerator}
of the \textsc{FastJet} contrib~\cite{Dreyer:2018nbf}, which follows the harder branch at each step
and returns the ordered list of primary declusterings. For
each declustering it provides the angular separation $\dR$, the relative
transverse momentum $\kt$, the momentum fraction $z$ and the Lund coordinates of
equation \eqref{eq:lund-coords}. Each primary declusterings in order to be recorded has to pass the 
SoftDrop condition of equation \ref{eq:softdrop} with $z_{\text{cut}} = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0$,
reducing it to $z > 0.1$. With this list of primary declusterings, the primary splitting is
chosen by selecting the declustering with largest $\kt$, with its harder and softer branches
denoted as $p_1$ and $p_2$. The softer branche $p_2$ is then declustered in the same way,
producing a second list of secondary declusterings. These secondary declusterings that satisfy, again,
the SoftDrop condition, form the secondary Lund plane, and the one with largest $\kt$ is selected
as the secondary splitting, with harder and softer branches denoted as $p_3$ and $p_4$. If among
the list of primary or secondary declusterings noone satisfies the SoftDrop condition, the jet
is flagged as a failure and no $\dpsi$ is computed.

Following the selection of the primary and secondary splittings, the azimuthal angle between
the two splitting planes is computed from the three-momenta of the four branches,
following the convention of the CMS measurement \cite{CMS:2026jnt}. The normals of the two planes
are

\begin{equation}
  \hat{n}_1 = \frac{\vec{p}_1 \times \vec{p}_2}{|\vec{p}_1 \times \vec{p}_2|},
  \qquad
  \hat{n}_2 = \frac{\vec{p}_3 \times \vec{p}_4}{|\vec{p}_3 \times \vec{p}_4|},
  \label{eq:normals}
\end{equation}

and the angle between them is

\begin{equation}
  \dpsi = \arccos\!\left( s \, \hat{n}_1 \cdot \hat{n}_2 \right),
  \qquad
  s = \operatorname{sign}\!\left[ (\hat{n}_1 \times \hat{n}_2) \cdot
      \vec{p}_{\mathrm{h}} \right],
  \label{eq:dpsi-impl}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{p}_{\mathrm{h}}$ is the more energetic of $\vec{p}_1$ and
$\vec{p}_2$. The sign $s$ orients the two normals consistently, so that $\dpsi$
is defined in the range $[0, \pi]$. Since the two branches of a declustering are always separated in angle, both
planes are well defined. Nevertheless, as a safe guard, the script returns no value in the
case of exactly collinear branches, for which the cross products of
equation~\eqref{eq:normals} vanish.

To conclude this process, a set of shape variables is computed for each of the four branches
$p_1$ to $p_4$ from the constituents it contains. hese are the number of
constituents $n_{\text{all}}$, the number of charged constituents
$n_{\text{ch}}$, the transverse-momentum-weighted width $\sigma$ and the
transverse-momentum-weighted mean constituent momentum $\langle \pt \rangle$,
defined as

\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{\sum_i p_{\mathrm{T},i}\,\Delta R_i}{\sum_i p_{\mathrm{T},i}},
  \qquad
  \langle \pt \rangle = \frac{\sum_i p_{\mathrm{T},i}^{2}}{\sum_i p_{\mathrm{T},i}},
  \label{eq:shapes}
\end{equation}

where the sums run over the constituents of the branch and $\Delta R_i$ is the
angular distance of constituent $i$ from the branch axis. These variables play no role
in the computation of $\dpsi$. They characterise the internal structure of
each branch, which differs between branches initiated by quarks and by gluons, and
they serve as the inputs to the flavour selection of Section~\ref{sec:flavour}. In collision
data no truth information is available, so the channel of the second splitting is left undetermined
at this stage. The same computation, together with the determination of the splitting channel, is 
carried out in simulation data and it is described on the following section.

\subsection{Generator-level channel labelling}
\label{sec:genlevel}

In simulated data, the reconstructed jets are processed exactly as described in Section \ref{sec:declustering-impl},
so that the Lund observables obtained are identical to the ones obtained in collision data,
however, the channel label of the primary and secondary splittings is also assigned. This 
label is built from the final-state partons stored during extraction and its construction
is made in three steps. Flavoured jets are first built from the
partons, these generator-level jets are then matched to the reconstructed jets,
and finally the splittings of the two are matched and labelled.

Firstly, the final-state partons of each event are converted into four-momenta in the same 
way as the reconstructed constituents, through equation \eqref{eq:cartesian} and \eqref{eq:energy},
but, in addition, each parton is also assigned a flavour from their identity. A quark
carries one unit of its flavour, an antiquark minus one unit, and a gluon is
flavourless. The partons are then clustured using the \antikt algorithm with $R=0.8$, mimicking
the same definition as the reconstructed jets. Afterwards, it is necessary to match these new generated jets
with their respective reconstructed jet. This step is performed in their C/A reclustering forms.
On the reconstructed side, this is the object
obtained in Section~\ref{sec:declustering-impl} by reclustering the constituents
of the jet with the Cambridge/Aachen algorithm and $R = 2$. On the generator side,
the partons of each anti-$\kt$ jet containing at least two partons are reclustered
in the same way, with the Cambridge/Aachen algorithm and $R = 2$, combined with the
IFN procedure of Section~\ref{sec:ifn}~\cite{caola2023flavouredjetsexactantikt} with its parameter
$\alpha$ set to $2$. Each reconstructed jet, taken in order of decreasing
transverse momentum, is matched to the closest generator-level object in the
$(\eta,\phi)$ plane among those not already matched, provided that

\begin{equation}
  \dR\!\left( j^{\text{reco}}_{\text{C/A}},\, j^{\text{gen}}_{\text{C/A}} \right)
  < 0.4 ,
  \label{eq:jetmatch}
\end{equation}

a threshold equal to half the radius of the anti-$\kt$ jets. This matching process is therefore
one to one. Since each C/A object contains all the constituents of the \antikt jet which was built
from, its axis should coincide with the axis of the original jet, making it equivalent to matching
to matching the \antikt jets themselves. The generator-level object selected in this way
provides a Cambridge/Aachen history in which every branch carries an infrared-
and collinear-safe flavour. A reconstructed jet with no generator-level
counterpart satisfying equation~\eqref{eq:jetmatch} receives no truth label and is not
used in the training of the flavour selection.

With the jet matching complete, the generator-level history is then declustered via the same
process of as the reconstructed one. The harder branch is followed at each step, each
splitting is tested against the condition $z > 0.1$, and among the splittings that
satisfy it the one with the largest $\kt$ is taken as the primary generator-level
splitting. Its softer branch is declustered in turn to obtain the secondary
generator-level splitting. For each of these two splittings, the flavours of their branches
are read from the IFN history and the channel of their splitting is labelled according to 
Table \ref{tab:channels}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Assignment of the splitting channel from the IFN flavours of the two
  branches of a generator-level splitting.}
  \label{tab:channels}
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    Flavours of the two branches & Channel \\
    \midrule
    both flavourless                                   & $g \to gg$ \\
    one quark or antiquark and one flavourless         & $q \to qg$ \\
    a quark and an antiquark of the same flavour       & $g \to q\bar{q}$ \\
    any other combination                              & rest \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Note that during this work, the channel of a splitting is denoted as the one inferred from the
IFN flavours of its own branches thus, and, although it is an infrared and collinear safe
proxy, it may not coincide with coincide with the real truth channel of the splitting.
The flavour of a branch reflects
the whole of the radiation it contains, and the matching to the reconstructed jet
and the hadronisation introduce further differences. Therefore, the modulation obtained for a
given label in simulation does not reproduce the theoretical value of \cite{Karlberg_2021} 
for the corresponding channel.

In order to then label the primary and secondary reconstructed splittings, 
the primary and secondary generator-level splitting have to correspond geometrically to them.
For a reconstructed splitting with branches $p_a$
and $p_b$ and opening angle $\Delta_{ab}$, and the generator-level splitting with
branches $g_a$ and $g_b$, the requirement is

\begin{equation}
  \dR(p_a, g_a) < \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{ab}
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \dR(p_b, g_b) < \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{ab},
  \label{eq:splitmatch}
\end{equation}

where the same condition is also accepted with $g_a$ and $g_b$ interchanged. This is applied 
seperately to the primary splitting, with branches $p_1$ and $p_2$, and to the secondary
splitting with branches $p_3$ and $p_4$. When this condition is satisfied, the reconstructed
splitting gets labelled with the channel of Table \ref{tab:channels}. When the condition fails
to give any channel, the splitting is labelled as unmatched. An unmatched splitting is
one for which the reconstructed declustering does not reproduce the partonic
branching, typically because hadronisation or detector effects have altered the
clustering sequence. This type of splitting carries no relieable flavour information,
so it acts as a background which must be rejected.

Subsequently, each simulated jet carries a label for its first and second splittings, taking one of the values unmatched, $g \to q\bar{q}$, $q \to qg$, $g \to gg$ or
rest. The label of the second splitting is the truth information on which the
flavour selection of Section~\ref{sec:flavour} is trained. It is used only as the
target of that selection, while its inputs are the reconstructed quantities of
Section~\ref{sec:declustering-impl}, which are available in data as well.

\subsection{The energy correlator}
\label{sec:eeec-impl}

The three-point energy correlator of Section~\ref{sec:spin-eeec} is computed
separately from the declustering, using directly the jet constituents stored after
the extraction. This process does not involve any declustering or grooming, so
that it probes the spin correlation through a different route from the Lund
observables. It is evaluated both for all jets and for the jets selected by the
flavour selection of Section~\ref{sec:flavour}. The definition of the three-point
energy correlator follows that of Ref.~\cite{Karlberg_2021}, based on the original
proposal of Ref.~\cite{Chen_2021}, with the particle energies replaced by
transverse momenta and the opening angles between particles replaced by their
distances in the $(\eta,\phi)$ plane, as appropriate for a hadron collider.

For each jet, its constituents with non-zero transverse momentum are converted
into three-momenta through equation~\eqref{eq:cartesian}, and jets with fewer than three
such constituents are skipped. The angular distance between every pair of
constituents is computed as
%
\begin{equation}
  \dR_{ij} = \sqrt{(\eta_i - \eta_j)^2 + (\phi_i - \phi_j)^2},
  \label{eq:eeec-dr}
\end{equation}
%
with the azimuthal difference computed in the range $(-\pi,\pi]$. Again, this
distance is invariant under boosts along the beam axis, making it the natural
measure of angular separation at a hadron collider, and it is the same measure used
to define the jets themselves. An opening angle in three dimensions would instead
shrink with the pseudorapidity of the jet, so that a fixed window in that angle
would select different regions of the jet for central and forward jets.

This computation then runs over every unordered triplet of constituents. The three
distances of a triplet are ordered as $\dR_S \leq \dR_M \leq \dR_L$, and the
triplet is retained only if
%
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{0.1} < \dR_L < 1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  0.01 < \dR_S < 0.1 .
  \label{eq:eeec-window}
\end{equation}
%
These are the values used for the opening angles in Ref.~\cite{Karlberg_2021},
but for the $(\eta,\phi)$ plane. These two definitions coincide at small angles for
central jets. The window selects configurations with a strong hierarchy of angles.
Two of the constituents lie close together and resolve a narrow splitting, while
the third lies at a much wider angle. This configuration then approximates two
successive splittings with strongly ordered opening angles, which is the regime
where the correlator is sensitive to the spin of the intermediate gluon.

For any retained triplet, let $a$ and $b$ be the two constituents separated by the
smallest distance $\dR_S$ and $c$ the third. The azimuthal angle $\psi$ is the
angle between the plane containing $\vec{p}_a$ and $\vec{p}_b$ and the plane
containing their sum and $\vec{p}_c$. It is computed from the three-momenta of
equation~\eqref{eq:cartesian}, through the normals
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{n}_1 = \frac{\vec{p}_a \times \vec{p}_b}{|\vec{p}_a \times \vec{p}_b|},
  \qquad
  \hat{n}_2 = \frac{(\vec{p}_a + \vec{p}_b) \times \vec{p}_c}
                   {|(\vec{p}_a + \vec{p}_b) \times \vec{p}_c|},
  \label{eq:eeec-normals}
\end{equation}
%
as the signed angle $\psi\in(-\pi,\pi]$ defined by
\begin{equation}
  \cos\psi = \hat{n}_1\cdot\hat{n}_2,
  \qquad
  \sin\psi = \hat{u}\cdot(\hat{n}_1\times\hat{n}_2),
  \qquad
  \hat{u} = \frac{\vec{p}_a+\vec{p}_b}
                 {|\vec{p}_a+\vec{p}_b|}.
  \label{eq:eeec-psi}
\end{equation}
Since both normals are perpendicular to $\hat{u}$, these two
relations fix $\psi$ uniquely, its sign being given by the sense
of the rotation from $\hat{n}_1$ to $\hat{n}_2$ about $\hat{u}$. Exchanging $a$ and $b$
will reverse $\hat{n}_1$ and shift $\psi$ by $\pi$, leaving $\cos 2\psi$ unchanged.
The two constituents are taken in the order in which they are stored. Each triplet
enters the distribution of $\psi$ with the weight
%
\begin{equation}
  w_{abc} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{T},a}\,p_{\mathrm{T},b}\,p_{\mathrm{T},c}}
                 {p_{\mathrm{T},\text{jet}}^{3}} ,
  \label{eq:eeec-weight}
\end{equation}
%
in accordance with equation~\eqref{eq:eeec}. Since the sum runs over unordered
triplets, it will differ from a sum over all orderings of $i$, $j$ and $k$ only by
a constant factor. This will affect the normalisation of the distribution, but not
its shape and therefore it will not affect the factor $a_2/a_0$. The weighted
distribution is accumulated in a histogram of $100$ bins spanning $(-\pi,\pi]$. In
the simulated datasets, the distribution is also accumulated separately for jets in
each channel of the second splitting, as labelled in Section~\ref{sec:genlevel},
which provides a channel-by-channel reference for the modulation. Since the
triplets of a jet share its constituents, they are not independent, and the
statistical uncertainties are computed treating the jets as the independent units.

The main difficulty of this stage is the computational cost, since each jet may
have many constituents and the number of triplets grows as the cube of their
number. To make the computation manageable, the pairwise distances are computed
once per jet, the window of equation~\eqref{eq:eeec-window} is applied before any other
quantity is evaluated, so that most triplets are rejected at negligible cost, and
the loop over triplets is compiled to machine code with the \textsc{Numba}
just-in-time compiler~\cite{Lam:2015numba}. The correlator of each jet is also
computed only once and added to every category of jets to which the jet belongs,
and the events are processed in parallel in independent blocks.

\section{Flavour selection}
\label{sec:flavour}

The core measurement of this work requires isolating the secondary splitting channel $g \to q\bar{q}$.
As discussed in Section \ref{sec:spin-channel}, the modulation of this channel carries 
opposite sign compared to the $g \to gg$, so that an inclusive measurement dilutes the effect,
while the other channel $q \to qg$ vanishes. However, the channel of a splitting is only
defined in simulation, through generator-level labelling of Section~\ref{sec:genlevel}, while
in collision data remains undefined.

The approach adopted here in this section is to learn, in simulation, the relation between
this true channel and quantities that are measured in the same way in data. For that,
a classifier was trained on the reconstructed features of each jet, with the truth
label as its target, and is then applied unchanged to the collision data. An important
feature is that its output is not a definite label but a score, whose value gives how likely
a certain splitting is to belong to the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel. With that score, one 
can require it to exceed a threshold and obtain an enriched sample of that channel.
The $\dpsi$ observable is then measured as a function of this threshold,
with the fraction of $g \to q\bar{q}$ splittings in each selected sample, its
purity, determined from simulation.

This section is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:flavour-strategy} describes
the overall strategy and the classes into which the splittings are divided.
Section~\ref{sec:flavour-features} presents the input features of the classifier,
and Section~\ref{sec:flavour-training} the architecture of the networks and the
procedure used to train them. Section~\ref{sec:flavour-alternatives} describes
the alternative approaches that were considered, and
Section~\ref{sec:flavour-application} explains how the trained networks are
applied to the data and how the purity of the selected samples is computed in
simulation.

\subsection{Strategy and classes}
\label{sec:flavour-strategy}

The generator level-labelling of section \ref{sec:genlevel} divides the simulated jets
into five classes according to their secondary splitting channel. A splitting can either be unmatched,
if the reconstructed declustering does not reproduce the partonic
branching, or be assigned with the channels $g \to q\bar{q}$, $q \to qg$, $g \to gg$, or with a residual class, denoted as rest.
The composition of the simulated sample in these classes is given in
Table~\ref{tab:composition}. As one can notice, the unmatched label represents the 
majority of the dataset, about three quarters of it, but it does carry no relieable flavour
information, while, among the matched splittings, the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel is a minority,
outnumbered by both $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$.

The selection procedure is therefore divided into two stages, each adressing a part of the dataset.
The first stage tries to seperate the matched splittings, the channels
$g \to q\bar{q}$, $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$, from the unmatched ones. Its output is a matched score,
which is the classifier's confidence level that it is a matched labelled channel. Only the 
jets with matched score exceeding $0.8$ are passed to the
second stage. The second stage is only trained on matched splittings and seperate the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel from the $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$ channels
taken together. This classifier's output is the $g \to q\bar{q}$ score on which the threshold of
the measurement is placed. As suggested in \cite{CMS:2026jnt}, the residual cattegory is 
excluded from the training of both stages, since it represents less than one percent of the dataset
and represents other flavour configurations that do not correspond to any channel.

Dividing the problem in this way makes the two classifiers concentrate on a single binary question.
The first stage removes the unmatched background, in which the
flavour label is not useful.  The second stage then has to solve only the
separation that matters for the measurement, without spending capacity on the
distinction between $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$, which plays no role in the
selection. The strong imbalance between the classes in both stages is compensated
during training, as described in Section~\ref{sec:flavour-training}.

\subsection{Input features}
\label{sec:flavour-features}

The inputs of the classifers are built from the primary and secondary splittings of each jet and
from the four branches computed in section \ref{sec:declustering-impl}. They fall
into two groups, the shapes of the branches of the second splitting and the
kinematics of the two selected splittings, and are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:features}.

Each of these variables exploit the different radiation patterns of quark and gluons.
A gluon carries the larger colour factor, $C_A = 3$ against $C_F = 4/3$ for a
quark, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:qcd}, and therefore radiates more. Because
of that, a branch initiated by a gluon tends to have more constituents, to be wider, and 
to share its momentum among softer constituents than a branch initiated by a
quark. For each of the two branches $p_3$ and $p_4$ of the secondary splitting, this
is captured by the number of constituents $n_{\text{all}}$, the number of charged
constituents $n_{\text{ch}}$, the width $\sigma$ and the weighted mean constituent
momentum $\langle \pt \rangle$ of equation \eqref{eq:shapes}. This is supposed 
to capture the nature of these two branches. A $g \to q\bar{q}$ splitting
produces two quark branches, a $g \to gg$ splitting two gluon branches, and a
$q \to qg$ splitting one of each.

The kinematic variables exploit the different momentum sharing of the channels. From the splitting
functions of equations \eqref{eq:Pgg} and \eqref{eq:Pqq}, it can be noted that the channels
$g \to gg$ and $q \to qg$ have increased probability when one of the two products is soft, while
the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel of equation \eqref{eq:Pgq}, has increased probability with a more even
sharing of the momentum. The momentum fraction $z_2$ of the secondary splitting is, therefore,
sensitive to its channel and it is shown in section \ref{sec:res-zplane} precisely that. 
It is also complemented by the angular separation
$\dR_{34}$ and the relative transverse momentum $k_{\mathrm{T},2}$ of the second
splitting, and by the momentum fraction $z_1$ of the first.

The first stage uses these twelve variables directly while the second stage is also supplemented 
by combinations that do not depend on the ordering of the two
branches. For each pair of variables and for he pair $z_1$ and $z_2$, the sum 
the absolute difference, the minimum, the maximum and the ratio of the minimum to the maximum 
are computed and added to the second classifier. This is motivated by the symmetry of 
the problem. Since in a $g \to q\bar{q}$ splitting both branches are quarks and in a 
$q \to qg$ splitting the quark is either the harder or the softer branch, this should help
the classifier see the flavour information as a whole and not by its ordering. 
In the second stage the variables $\langle \pt \rangle$ and $k_{\mathrm{T},2}$,
whose distributions have long tails, are also replaced by their logarithms,
$\ln(1 + x)$. In both stages every feature is finally standardised to zero mean
and unit variance, using the statistics of the training sample.

Moreover, to avoid biasing the measurement, two quantities available for each jet
are excluded from the inputs. The first is the observable itself, $\dpsi$. A
classifier with access to it could select jets according to their azimuthal angle,
and would thereby shape the very distribution that this work measures. The second
is the transverse momentum of the jet. The flavour composition of the sample
changes with the jet transverse momentum, and the selection is trained and
calibrated in different intervals of $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}}$. Excluding the jet
transverse momentum prevents the classifier from learning this composition, and
keeps its output tied to the internal structure of the splitting rather than to
the kinematics of the jet. It also allows the same classifier to be applied, in
future work, to jets in other ranges of transverse momentum.

\subsection{Network architecture and training}
\label{sec:flavour-training}

The two stages of the selection are feedforward neural networks, implemented with Keras~\cite{Chollet:2015keras} on top of
TensorFlow~\cite{Abadi:2016tf}, with the preprocessing of the inputs performed
with scikit-learn~\cite{Pedregosa:2011sklearn}. The networks share the same structure 
with three hidden layers of $256$, $128$ and $64$ neurons with
rectified linear activations, and is regularised through a penalty on the squared
magnitude of its weights, batch normalisation and dropout. Their differences are
the strength of the regularisation, in their output layer and in the loss function
minimised during training. The complete configuration of the two networks is
given in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Architecture and training configuration of the two stages of the
  flavour selection.}
  \label{tab:hyperparameters}
  \begin{tabular}{llrr}
    \toprule
    & Stage 1 & Stage 2 \\
    \midrule
    Hidden layers        & $256$, $128$, $64$         & $256$, $128$, $64$ \\
    Activation           & ReLU                       & ReLU \\
    L2 penalty           & $10^{-4}$                  & $10^{-5}$ \\
    Batch normalisation  & after every layer          & after every layer \\
    Dropout              & $0.25$, $0.20$, $0.10$     & $0.20$, $0.20$, $0.10$ \\
    Output               & two neurons, softmax       & one neuron, sigmoid \\
    Loss                 & categorical cross-entropy  & binary focal loss \\
    Class weights        & balanced, power $0.75$     & balanced \\
    Optimiser            & Adam, rate $10^{-3}$       & Adam, rate $10^{-3}$ \\
    Batch size           & $1024$                     & $1024$ \\
    Maximum epochs       & $100$                      & $300$ \\
    Monitored metric     & macro-averaged F1 score    & area under the PR curve \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The first stage neural network has two output neurons with a softmax activation, thus
returning the probabilities that the secondary splitting is matched or unmatched, and the first
one is the matched score used in this selection. This network was trained by minimising 
the categorical cross-entropy and, since the matched splittings make about three quarters of 
the dataset, each class is weighted in the loss by a factor inversely
proportional to its frequency. These weights are softened by raising them to the
power $0.75$, which prevents the network from being dominated by the majority
class without overcompensating for it.

The second stage has a single output neuron with a sigmoid activation, whose value
is the $g \to q\bar{q}$ score. It is trained with the binary focal
loss~\cite{Lin:2017focal},

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} = -\,\alpha_t \left( 1 - p_t \right)^{\gamma}
  \ln p_t ,
  \label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}

where $p_t$ is the probability that the network assigns to the correct class, and
$\alpha_t$ equals $\alpha$ for splittings of the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel and
$1-\alpha$ for the others. The values $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.5$ are used. The
factor $(1-p_t)^{\gamma}$ reduces the contribution of splittings that are already
classified with confidence, and concentrates the training on the ambiguous ones.
This is very fitting to the problem since the channels overlap each other in the 
space of the input features and most splittings are hard to classify. Balanced
class weights are applied furthermore, to compensate for the minority of the
$g \to q\bar{q}$ channel among the other splittings.

The difference between the two networks is due to the different nature of their task.
The first stage network seperates classes which differ more clearly in the kinematics 
of the splittings, since unmatched splittings are those in which the
reconstructed branching departs from the partonic one, and the separation is
learned with relative ease. Therefore, this model has a stronger regularisation,
with larger weight penalty and more dropout in its first layer, preventing it from
learning accidental patterns or noise that exists only in the training dataset and 
encouraging it to more general patterns that are more likely to work on general unseen data.
Meanwhile, the second stage must instead extract the smaller
differences between the shapes of quark and gluon branches. It uses lighter 
regularisation and a longer maximum training time so it can learn these weaker 
features, while early stopping prevents overtraining in both cases. The class 
imbalance treatment also differs. In the first stage, neither class is of particular 
interest, and the softened weights keep the matched score closer to a probability, so 
that the fixed requirement of $0.8$ retains its meaning. In the second stage the underrepresented 
group is the one of interest, and fully balanced weights ensure that it is not neglected. 
Finally, the metric used to select the best epoch matches how each output is used. The 
macro-averaged F1 score weights the two classes of the first stage equally, while the 
accuracy of the second stage isprecisely the purity of the selected sample, so that the 
area under the precision--recall curve measures directly the purity achievable at each efficiency.

Both networks are trained on the simulated dataset with
$800 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 1000$~GeV, using the jets whose secondary splitting
have been labelled, but excluding the residual class. The first stage network was trained 
on all such jets while the second stage network only on the matched ones. The sample is
divided into training, validation and test subsets containing about two thirds,
one sixth and one sixth of the events. The division was made by event, so that the
jets of a given event never appear in different subsets. This step was also tested
with a division made by jet and the performance was essentially the same, which was expected
since the jets supposably originate from independent showers. The preprocessing of
Section~\ref{sec:flavour-features}, is determined on the
training subset alone and then applied unchanged to the other subsets and to the
data.

The networks are optimised with the Adam algorithm~\cite{Kingma:2014adam} in
batches of $1024$ jets. After each epoch, the performance is evaluated on the
validation subset, using the macro-averaged F1 score for the first stage and the
area under the precision-recall curve for the second. The latter is better suited
than the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve to a problem in which the class of interest is a
minority. The learning rate is halved whenever this metric fails to improve for
five consecutive epochs, down to a minimum of $10^{-6}$, and training stops after
fifteen epochs without improvement, at which point the weights of the best epoch
are restored. The test subset is used only for the final evaluation of the
networks.

\subsection{Alternative approaches}
\label{sec:flavour-alternatives}

The double stage selection described in the previous sections was used after comparison 
with three alternative strategies. All these different approaches were built on the same
input features and the same preprocessing, and, again, they all exclude the 
$\dpsi$ variable from their inputs.

The first alternative is a single neural network that classifies each jet into the 
four categories unmatched, $g \to q\bar{q}$, $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$.
It's structure is the same as of the double stage selection with the same hidden layers 
described in Table \ref{tab:hyperparameters}, a
softmax output with four neurons, and is trained with the categorical
cross-entropy and class weights softened with the power $0.75$. This strategy attempts 
to solve the two problems that the two stage neural networks approach individually. 
A network of this kind is used by CMS in \cite{CMS:2026jnt}, where it was trained on 
a different generator and it tries to 
reject the unmatched splittings and identify the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel.

The second approach replaces the binary second stage by a network with three
outputs, trained on the matched splittings to separate $g \to q\bar{q}$,
$q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$, with the same loss and class weights as the first
alternative. It is applied after the same first stage, and its $g \to q\bar{q}$
output is used for the selection.

The third alternative is an unsupervised neural network. The matched splittings are described by the
same features, reduced with a principal component analysis, and a Gaussian mixture
model with full covariance matrices is fitted to them for several numbers of
components, without any use of the flavour labels. The resulting clusters are then compared
with the channel composition, in order to test whether the structure of
the feature space separates the channels by itself.

Although these approaches seem fair, none of them could seperate the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel as effectively as
the two-stage selection, in an high purity region, as shown in Chapter~\ref{cap:resultados}. The reason behind this
may be because the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel is a minority
of the sample, and a network that must also seperate the channels $q \to qg$ and $g \to gg$,
or reject the unmatched splittings at the same time, spends part of its power on that 
which it is not required on this measurement. 
In the unsupervised case, the channels overlap
strongly in the space of the input features, so that there is no reason for the
clusters of a Gaussian mixture to align with them, and the clusters found indeed
mix the channels. The two-stage selection is therefore the one used in the
remainder of this work.

\subsection{Application and purity calibration}
\label{sec:flavour-application}

After training, the two stage networks are applied unchanged to every jet with a valid
$\dpsi$, both in the collision data and in the simulated calibration sample. The
inputs of each jet are built and preprocessed exactly as in training, using the
same engineered features and the same imputation and standardisation, determined
on the training subset. Thus, each jet receives two scores, the matched score of
the first stage and the $g \to q\bar{q}$ score of the second, which are then stored
alongside the other jet quantities.

Afterwards, the selection is made by picking jets with a matched score
above $0.8$, and a $g \to q\bar{q}$ score above a threshold $t$. The first requirement
is fixed and removes most of the unmatched splittings. The threshold
$t$ varies, and each of its values defines a selected sample. As $t$
increases, the samples become smaller and progressively enriched in the
$g \to q\bar{q}$ channel.

For each threshold $t$, the composition of the selected sample is computed on the simulated
sample with $600 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 800$~GeV, which is independent
of the one used in training. The purity and the efficiency of the selection are
defined as

\begin{equation}
  P(t) = \frac{N_{q\bar{q}}(t)}{N(t)},
  \qquad
  \varepsilon(t) = \frac{N_{q\bar{q}}(t)}{N_{q\bar{q}}},
  \label{eq:purity-efficiency}
\end{equation}

where $N(t)$ is the number of selected jets, $N_{q\bar{q}}(t)$ the number of them
whose secondary splitting is labelled $g \to q\bar{q}$, and $N_{q\bar{q}}$ the total
number of jets carrying that label before the selection. When increasing the threshold,
the purity grows while the efficiency declines, and the comparison between the two
is shown in Chapter \ref{cap:resultados} summarising the performance of the selection.

At each threshold, the mean value $\langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle$ of
equation \eqref{eq:mean-cos} is computed on the selected jets, seperately in data and simulation,
with the statistical uncertainty of equation \eqref{eq:stat}. The measurement made is presented
as a function of the threshold, with the purity of equation \eqref{eq:purity-efficiency} given for each value.
The two reference are computed from simulation and data are the mean over all jets labelled $g \to q\bar{q}$ and the
mean over those labelled $q \to qg$ or $g \to gg$, which indicates the value expected 
for the labelled channel and for the background before any selection.
The evaluated threshold range is from $0.3$ up to $0.6$ and beyond this value the number
of jets fall below a few hundred and the relative statistical
uncertainty exceeds $10\%$, making these points carry little
information.

The main assumption of this procedure is that the purity computed in simulation samples also
describes the samples selected from data at the same threshold. The
networks are identical in the two cases, but the composition of the selected
samples depends on how well the simulation reproduces the data, as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:sys-purity}.

\section{Systematic uncertainties}
\label{sec:systematic}

This work's quantity is the mean value $\langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle$, given per threshold of 
the $g \to q\bar{q}$ score organized in samples, accompanied by the purity assigned to each
sample, according to simulation. The $\langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle$ and purity quantities are both
subject of uncertanties. The statistical uncertanty comes from the finite number of jets 
in each sample and it is evaluated at every point of this measurement. The systematic 
uncertanties arise from the modelling of the simulation on which the selection is trained and
calibrated, from the response of the detector, and from some choices made in this analysis. 
However, unlike the statistical uncertainties, they do not decrease when extracting more jets
and they may shift the result in a definite result

A complete quantitive evaluation of every systematic uncertainty was not done in this work.
This section just identifies the relevant souces to the measurement, describes how each of them
is managed by this analysis, and states which ones are not evaluated. Section~\ref{sec:sys-stat} describes the statistical uncertainty.
Section~\ref{sec:sys-purity} discusses the modelling of the purity, which is the
dominant systematic source. Section~\ref{sec:sys-detector} addresses the effects
of the detector, and Section~\ref{sec:sys-choices} the trigger and the choices of
the analysis. An overview of the sources considered and of the treatment of each is given in
Table~\ref{tab:systematics}, and each source is discussed in turn in the
subsections that follow.

\subsection{Statistical uncertainty}
\label{sec:sys-stat}

For a selected sample of $N$ jets, the measured quantity is the mean computed as

\begin{equation}
  \langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \cos 2\dpsi^{(j)},
  \label{eq:mean-cos}
\end{equation}

and its statistical uncertainty is the standard error of the mean,

\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\text{stat}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}},
  \qquad
  s^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
        \left( \cos 2\dpsi^{(j)} - \langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle \right)^2 .
  \label{eq:stat}
\end{equation}

For a distribution of the form of equation \eqref{eq:dpsi-modulation}, the mean value
is $\langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}\,a_2/a_0$, so that the uncertainty on
$a_2/a_0$ is twice that of equation~\eqref{eq:stat}.

This expression assumes that each jet entering the sample is independent. In each event after selection,
there is at least two jets and all of them enter the analysis, although, events with 
more than two jets are rare. In that case, the jets of an event originate from separate showers, and their second splittings
are essentially uncorrelated. This is statement is supported by the behaviour of the flavour
selection, because its performance did not change, when training and test samples were
separated by event rather than by jet.

The purity given for each sample is the fraction of selected simulated jets whose second secondary
splitting is labelled $g \to q\bar{q}$ by the IFN procedure. Its
statistical uncertainty follows from the binomial distribution,

\begin{equation}
  \delta P = \sqrt{\frac{P\,(1-P)}{N_{\text{MC}}}},
  \label{eq:purity-stat}
\end{equation}

where $N_{\text{MC}}$ is the number of selected simulated jets. The same
expression of equation \eqref{eq:stat} gives the statistical uncertainty of the mean
values obtained in simulation. In this work, when showing the results as a function
of the threshold, the threshold itself is an exact choice, given by the classifiers,
and carries no uncertanty, so that only the vertical uncertainties of equation \eqref{eq:stat} are presented.

There are two features of the statistical uncertainties that should be kept in mind while
reading the results. The first is that it grows with the threshold, since raising it
catches fewer jets, making the threshold's range limited by the number of jets that 
survive. The second is that each sample is a subset of the
samples selected at lower thresholds, so that consecutive points share most of
their jets and their fluctuations are correlated. A trend across thresholds should
therefore be judged against the uncertainty of the individual points rather than
against the smoothness of the curve.

\subsection{Modelling of the purity}
\label{sec:sys-purity}

As said on the previous subsection, the purity is the fraction of the selected simulated jets whose second secondary
splitting is labelled $g \to q\bar{q}$ by the IFN procedure. It
is taken from the simulation and transferred to the data, and it is therefore
correct for the data only if the simulation reproduces two of
their features. The first is the flavour compostion of the secondary splitting, in particular
the rate of $g \to q\bar{q}$ splittings relative to $q \to qg$ and
$g \to gg$. The second is the distribution of the input features of
Table \ref{tab:features} within each channel. If one of them is mismodelled,
the same threshold selects a sample of different purity in data than in simulation.

There is direct evidence that this effect is not negligible. In the CMS
measurement on which this analysis is based~\cite{CMS:2026jnt}, neither
\textsc{Pythia}~8 nor \textsc{Herwig}~7 describes the distribution of the
classifier output in data, owing to the mismodelling of several input variables,
and the simulation is reweighted to reproduce the data before the purity is
determined, with an associated uncertainty derived from the difference in flavour
composition between the two generators. No such reweighting is applied in this
work, and the purities quoted are those of \textsc{Pythia}~8 with the CP5 tune. A
repetition of the calibration with a generator based on a different parton shower
and hadronisation model was not performed, and the modelling of the purity is
therefore the largest source of systematic uncertainty that is not quantified
here.

The labelling done in Section \ref{sec:flavour-strategy}, provides a partial consistency 
check. The composition showed in Table \ref{tab:composition} agrees closely with the 
one obtained in \cite{CMS:2026jnt}. This confirms that the labels are constructed correctly, but
it does not prove that the simulation describes in the data.

Another aspect concerning the purity is its depence on the kinematics of the jets.
The selection is trained on simulation with
$800 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 1000$~GeV and computed on the independent interval
$600 < \hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}} < 800$~GeV, which secures that the purity is not
biased by the training. Since $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is the transverse
momentum of the partonic hard process, each sample contains jets over a brader range
of transverse momenta than its nominal interval, and both jets above the selection threshold of $700$~GeV.
The distribution of jet
transverse momenta in a single interval of $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}}$ but still differs
from the data, which receives contributions from all intervals, weighted by their cross sections.
Although the jet transverse momentum is not a variable which the classifier uses, 
the relation between threshold and purity may
still vary with it, so that a calibration performed on a single interval may not
describe the full data sample exactly. Combining several intervals, each weighted
by its cross section, would reproduce the spectrum of the data and remove this
limitation.

In addition, the purity depends on the definition of the flavour label. In this work, it is defined
as the matching of the branches of the second splitting, $p_3$ and
$p_4$, to their generator-level counterparts. However, other choices are possible and, for 
example, the purities quoted in \cite{CMS:2026jnt} are obtained by matching the branches 
$p_2$ and $p_3$ instead, yielding far fewer unmatched splittings, thus a different result.
Purities obtained with different definitions are, of course, not directly
comparable. 

\subsection{Detector effects}
\label{sec:sys-detector}

This measurement is performed on reconstructed particles which are not corrected to the level
of stable particles, making every quantity affected by the finite resolution of the detector.
The most relevant effect for an angular observable is the resolution on the directions of the
constituents. For charged particles, the direction is measured by the inner tracker with high precision.
For neutral particles, it is determined only by the calorimeters, whose granularity in
the barrel corresponds to cells of about $0.0174 \times 0.0174$ in $(\eta,\phi)$ for
the electromagnetic calorimeter and $0.087 \times 0.087$ for the hadron
calorimeter~\cite{CMS:2008}. Splittings resolved at angles comparable to these sizes,
are thus measured less precisely which is particularly relevant for the lower
edge $\dR_S > 0.01$ of the window of the energy correlator. The smearing of the
directions blurs the orientation of the reconstructed planes and dilutes the
modulation, so that the measured values of $\langle \cos 2\dpsi \rangle$ are
expected to be somewhat smaller in magnitude than at particle level.

The simulation dataset is done with a full simulation of the CMS detector, so that data and simulation
are compared at the same level and this dilution is present in both. Therefore, comparing 
both the datasets is consistent, however the values obtained
are specific to the CMS detector and cannot be compared with calculations
at particle level. Such a comparison would require an unfolding of the detector
response, as performed in \cite{CMS:2026jnt}, which is not done in this work.

The jets come with the standard energy corrections done by CMS, whose residual uncertainty
moves jets across the selection threshold of $700$~GeV. Since the measured observable
is an angle averaged by many jets, and the jets near the threshold form a small part of
the sample, this effect is expected to be small, and it is not evaluated.

Particles from additional proton--proton interactions in the same bunch crossing,
known as pile-up, enter the jets as soft constituents spread over wide angles.
Part of this contribution is already removed in the reconstruction of the jets,
since charged particles originating from pile-up vertices are discarded before the
clustering, a procedure known as charged-hadron subtraction. Neutral pile-up
particles may nevertheless remain among the constituents, with their full momenta.
The CMS measurement goes further and also rescales the momenta of neutral
particles by their probability of originating from the primary
interaction~\cite{CMS:2026jnt}, which is not done in this work. The effect of the
remaining pile-up on the observable is limited by the condition $z > 0.1$ imposed
on the selected splittings, which rejects the soft branches in which pile-up
particles are mostly found. The simulated samples include an overlay of pile-up
interactions, so that its effect is present in data and simulation alike.

\subsection{Trigger and analysis choices}
\label{sec:sys-choices}

The trigger is not expected to introduce a significant uncertanty, since, in CMS,
a trigger requiring a jet with a transverse momentum above $550$~GeV
was found to be more than $99.5\%$ efficient for jets above
$700$~GeV~\cite{CMS:2026jnt}. Thus, the trigger paths used here in this analysis, are expected
to have almost full efficiency, but this efficiency is not measured in this work.

Regarding some analysis choices done along the development of this work, several parameters
were fixed. These are the grooming
condition $z > 0.1$ applied to the selected splittings, the requirement of a
matched score above $0.8$ in the first stage of the selection, the angular window
of the energy correlator, and the criteria used to match the generator-level jets
and splittings to the reconstructed ones. Playing with these conditions would probe 
the stability of this work's result, however such thing was not done here.

Of the sources discussed in this section, the two related to the purity are the
ones that most limit the interpretation of the result. They are the reason for
presenting the measurement as a function of the threshold, with the purity from
simulation given as additional information rather than as a calibrated axis. A
full evaluation of these sources, based on an alternative generator and on the
variations of the analysis choices listed above, is the most important extension
of the present analysis and is discussed in Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusoes}.
