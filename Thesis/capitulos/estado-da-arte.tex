\label{cap:estado-da-arte}

\section{Quantum Chromodynamics}
\label{sec:qcd}

Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) is the quantum field theory of the strong interaction,
a non-Abelian gauge theory built on the colour symmetry group $SU(3)$ \cite{Ellis:1996mzs}.
Its fundamental degrees of freedom are quarks, which carry colour charge, and gluons,
the gauge bosons that mediate the interaction. The non-Abelian character of the theory
has a decisive consequence absent from Quantum Electrodynamics. The gluons themselves
carry colour, and therefore couple directly to one another. To a large extent, the
dynamics of the strong interaction are the dynamics of this gluon self-coupling, and
the two properties of QCD that govern everything in this chapter both follow from it.

\subsection{Asymptotic freedom and confinement}

The first property is asymptotic freedom. The strong coupling $\alpha_s$ decreases
as the momentum transfer of an interaction increases, so that at the high scales 
probed in LHC collisions the quarks and gluons interact weakly and perturbation
theory applies \cite{Gross:1973id,Politzer:1973fx}. The running of the coupling
is captured at leading order by

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_s(Q^2) = \frac{\alpha_s(\mu^2)}
    {1 + \alpha_s(\mu^2)\,\dfrac{33 - 2 n_f}{12\pi}\,\ln\!\dfrac{Q^2}{\mu^2}},
    \label{eq:running}
\end{equation}

where $Q$ is the scale of the process, $\mu$ a reference scale, and $n_f$ the
number of active quark flavours. The positive sign of the coefficient
$33 - 2n_f$, which holds for any physical number of flavours, is what makes the
coupling fall at large $Q$.  It is a direct imprint of the gluon self-interaction,
and its discovery established that a perturbative treatment of hard processes is
justified. It is convenient to trade the reference scale for a single dimensionful
constant $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, the scale at which the perturbative coupling formally
diverges, of an order of a few hundred MeV. In terms of it, the coupling is small for
$Q \gg \Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$ and grows strong as $Q$ approaches it.

This growth is the second property called confinement. At low momentum transfer,
$\alpha_s$ becomes large and colour-charged particles cannot be isolated. A
quark or gluon produced in a hard scattering does not propagate freely to a detector,
because as it separates from other colour charges, the energy stored in the colour field
between them grows with distance until it becomes energetically favourable to create
a new quark-antiquark pairs from the vacuum. The original parton is thereby
dressed into a spray of colour-neutral hadrons. This is why the strongly interacting
final state reaches the detector not as partons but as the collimated sprays introduced
in Chapter \ref{cap:introducao}, and why any comparison between a perturbative
calculation and a measurement must bridge the divide between the partonic and hadronic
descriptions.

\subsection{Soft and collinear radiation}

The bridge between the hard parton and the observed hadrons is built from
successive emissions, and the pattern of those emissions is far from uniform.
When a parton radiates a gluon, the emission probability at first order in the
coupling takes the schematic form

\begin{equation}
  d\mathcal{P} \;\propto\; \frac{2\alpha_s C}{\pi}\,
  \frac{d\theta}{\theta}\,\frac{dE}{E},
  \label{eq:soft-collinear}
\end{equation}

where $E$ and $\theta$ are the energy and emission angle of the radiated gluon,
and $C$ is a colour factor. The two factors carry the characteristic structure of
QCD radiation. The factor $d\theta/\theta$ diverges as $\theta \to 0$ meaning that
emissions are enhanced when the gluon is radiated close to the direction of its emitter.
This is known as the collinear divergence. The factor $dE/E$ diverges as $E \to 0$
meaning that emissions are enhanced when the gluon is soft. This is known
as the soft divergence. Together they mean that a hard parton radiates predominantly
softly and at small angles, and that number of such emissions is,
in a precise logarithmic sense, large.

The collinear structure is described more completely by the 
Dokshitzer–Gribov–Lipatov–Altarelli–Parisi (DGLAP) splitting
functions \cite{Ellis:1996mzs}, which give the probability that a parton of one type splits into two,
as a function of the momentum fraction $z$ carried by one of the products. For
the three splittings relevant to this work they read, at leading order,

\begin{align}
  P_{q \to qg}(z) &= C_F\,\frac{1 + z^2}{1 - z}, \label{eq:Pqq}\\[4pt]
  P_{g \to gg}(z) &= C_A\,
    \left[ \frac{z}{1-z} + \frac{1-z}{z} + z(1-z) \right], \label{eq:Pgg}\\[4pt]
  P_{g \to q\bar{q}}(z) &= T_R\,\left[ z^2 + (1-z)^2 \right], \label{eq:Pgq}
\end{align}

with colour factors $C_F = 4/3$, $C_A = 3$ and $T_R = 1/2$. The distinction
between the gluon splitting channels of Eqs. \eqref{eq:Pgg} and \eqref{eq:Pgq},
one producing a pair of gluons and the other a quark-antiquark pair, will
prove central. The two channels differ not only in these rates but in the spin
structure of their emissions, and it is that difference which the spin
observables of Section \ref{sec:spin} are designed to probe.

These divergences are not a pathology. They are integrable, and in any physical,
infrared-safe observable the divergent real emissions cancel against
corresponding virtual corrections, leaving a finite result. This cancellation
is the requirement that shapes the very definition of a jet in Section \ref{sec:jatos}.
Their logarithmic structure, however, is physical and measurable, and it is exactly
the structure that the Lund jet plane of Section \ref{sec:lund} is built to expose.
By mapping each emission onto logarithmic axes of angle and transverse momentum,
it turns the double logarithmic enhancement of equation \eqref{eq:soft-collinear}
into an approximately uniform density, against which finer correlations stand out.

\subsection{Parton showers and hadronization}

Because a single hard parton typically radiates many times before hadronizing,
the emissions can be organised into a sequence ordered from the hardest,
widest-angle splittings down to the soft and collinear ones. This sequence
is what is known as a parton shower. Modern Monte Carlo event generators implement
this sequence probabilistically through the splitting functions of equations 
\eqref{eq:Pqq}-\eqref{eq:Pgq}, evolving each parton down from the hard 
scale of the collision to a low scale of order $1\GeV$, at which the perturbative
description breaks down, and confinement takes over. Below that scale, the partons
are converted into hadrons by phenomenological hadronization models, tuned to data
rather than derived from first principles.

This picture, a hard, perturbatively calculable core, dressed by a shower of
increasingly soft and collinear emissions and finally hadronized, underlies
the whole of the analysis that follows. The observables studied in this work are
built to read the perturbative shower through the hadrons that reach the
detector, and for this reason the measurement is deliberately confined to a hard
kinematic regime, where the perturbative component dominates, and non-perturbative
corrections are comparatively suppressed.

\section{Jets}
\label{sec:jatos}

As settled in Section \ref{sec:qcd}, a coloured parton produced in a hard
scattering does not reach the detector directly because it radiates
and hadronizes before arriving as a collimated spray of hadrons. A jet is then
an object, which by grouping those hadrons and combining their momentum back together,
inverts the process approximately and estimates the momentum of the original parton that
produced them. Therefore, a jet is a reconstructed physical object, built from a construction
algorithm, with properties that depend on this specific algorithm used to build it. This 
section sets out the requirement which any jet definition must satisfy, in order to
be usable in perturbative QCD, and three algorithms, used in this work and built on that requirement. 

\subsection{Jet algorithms and infrared-collinear safety}
\label{sec:ircsafety}

A jet is built using two components. A jet algorithm establishes which inputs,
from detector-level energy deposits, reconstructed particles, or, in a
calculation, partons, are grouped together into a jet. A recombination scheme
picks up those grouped inputs and specifies how their momenta is combined 
in order to compute the momentum of the jet. Across this work, the inputs are
the particle-flow candidates, described in Chapter \ref{cap:metodos},
and momenta are combined in the
$E$-scheme, in which four-momenta are simply added. This scheme is Lorentz invariant
and returns jets with non-zero mass.

Though, not every conceivable grouping prescription is admissible.
The necessary requirement, and the one that eliminates most of them is infrared and collinear (IRC) safety.
In order for an observable to be infrared safe is that its value most remain unchanged 
by the addition of an arbitrarily soft emission. For it to be collinear safe, this value
most also remain unchanged if one of its inputs is replaced by two collinear inputs
sharing its momentum. So, a jet algorithm is IRC safe when the set of jets it returns 
is stable under both of these operations. A soft particle must not create or destroy a jet and a
collinear splitting must not change how particles are assigned to jets.

This requirement is not optional because of what follows directly from Section \ref{sec:qcd}.
The emission probability of equation \eqref{eq:soft-collinear}
diverges in both the soft and the collinear limits. In any IRC-safe observable
these divergent real emissions cancel against equally divergent virtual corrections,
leaving a finite result. Opposingly, in an IRC-unsafe observable, that cancellation
is ruined and a fixed-order perturbative prediction returns infinity rather than an
finite number. An IRC-unsafe jet definition is therefore not merely inconvenient but it also cannot
be compared with perturbative QCD at all. Since the purpose of measuring jets is to
confront the theory with data, IRC safety is a precondition and not a refinement.

In the historical development of jet algorithms this criteria has been conclusive.
The first definitions that appeared were cone algorithms, which grouped particles
falling within a fixed angular radius of a candidate axis. These algorithms were
simple and seed-based and were not IRC safe because a soft particle could seed a new
candidate cone, or a collinear splitting could change which particles acted as seeds, thus
either these flaws could alter the resulting jets. A lot of effort was put in order to
fix them and eventually producing seedless variants that restored collinear safety.
Alternatively, another family of algorithms, the sequential recombination algorithms,
are IRC safe by construction and this work utilised them.

Since the same mechanism reappears when flavour is assigned to jets in Section \ref{sec:ifn},
it is worh making concrete how such a problem arises. Consider
a seed-based cone algorithm applied to an event with two hard particles whose
separation is slightly greater than the cone radius, so that each seeds its own
jet. By adding a single soft particle between them, it can seed a third cone 
that overlaps both, merging the two hard particles into one jet where before
there were only two, which, again, it is an infrared-unsafe change,
since an arbitrarily soft emission has altered the hard jets.
A collinear splitting produces the collinear-unsafe failure.
Replacing one hard seed by two collinear particles, each
carrying half its transverse momentum, may leave neither above the seed threshold,
so that a jet which existed before the splitting is absent after it.
Therefore, in both cases, the divergent emissions of equation \eqref{eq:soft-collinear} 
feed directly into the
jets themselves, and the perturbative prediction diverges with them.

Going back to the family introduced before, the sequential algorithms avoid both 
failures by never relying on seeds or on fixed geometric cones. In these algorithms,
jets are built by repeatedly merging inputs according to a distance measure, so that soft
or collinear additions change only the order in which already nearby inputs are combined
and never the final assignment of hard momentum jets. This structural difference is the 
reason why this entire family is IRC safe and it is why it is recommended
for any measurement to be compared with theory over the cone algorithms. 

A sequential recombination algorithm is defined by a distance measure between
each pair of inputs and between each input and the beam. The generalised-$\kt$
family \cite{Salam_2010} writes these as

\begin{align}
  d_{ij} &= \min\!\left( \pt{}_{,i}^{2p},\, \pt{}_{,j}^{2p} \right)
           \frac{\dR_{ij}^2}{R^2}, \label{eq:dij}\\[4pt]
  d_{iB} &= \pt{}_{,i}^{2p}, \label{eq:diB}
\end{align}

where $\dR_{ij}^2 = (y_i - y_j)^2 + (\phi_i - \phi_j)^2$ is the squared angular
separation of the inputs in rapidity $y$ and azimuth $\phi$, $R$ is the jet radius
parameter, and the integer $p$ selects the algorithm. The algorithm, at each step,
finds the smallest of all the distances. f it is a $d_{ij}$, the inputs $i$ and
$j$ are recombined into a single input. If it is a $d_{iB}$, the input $i$ is
declared a final jet and removed. This procedure repeats until no inputs remain.
The returned jets are IRC safe for any integer value of $p$, but the value
of $p$ changes extremely how the clustering proceeds. The three choices $p=-1$,
$p=0$ and $p=+1$ give, respectively, the \antikt{}, Cambridge/Aachen and $\kt$
algorithms, of which the first two are the tools this work relies on and are
described in the following subsections.

In all three of those algorithms, the parameter $R$ appears and sets the angular
reach of the clustering through the ratio $\dR_{ij}^2/R^2$ in equation \eqref{eq:dij}.
A smaller $R$ yields narrower jets that resolve nearby structure but also capture less of
a single parton radiation. A larger $R$ collects more of that radiation at the price of
admitting more contamination from unrelated soft activity. The most appropriate
choice depends on what we intend to use the jets for, and the two algorithms this work
relies on are used with different values of $R$ for correspondingly different
goals. This point is, again, taken up in Sections \ref{sec:antikt}
and \ref{sec:ca}.

\subsection{The anti-$\kt$ algorithm}
\label{sec:antikt}

The choice $p = -1$ in Eqs. \eqref{eq:dij} and \eqref{eq:diB} defines the \antikt{}
algorithm \cite{Cacciari_2008}. With this value the inter-particle distance becomes

\begin{equation}
  d_{ij} = \min\!\left( \frac{1}{\pt{}_{,i}^{2}},\, \frac{1}{\pt{}_{,j}^{2}} \right)
           \frac{\dR_{ij}^2}{R^2},
  \label{eq:antikt-distance}
\end{equation}

so that the distance scales with the inverse squared transverse momenta of
the inputs. This makes the smallest distances, and thus the first recombinations,
commit to the hardest particles. The clustering is therefore driven by the hard inputs.
So, a hard particle accumulates the softer inputs around it before soft inputs
cluster among themselves.

The name "anti" of this algorithm reflects this inversion. In the $\kt$ algorithm,
the one with the choice of $p=1$, the distance scales with the positive powers of
transverse momentum, making the soft unputs cluster first and the jet being assembled from
the bottom, following the inverse of the physical shower order. The \antikt{} algorithm
does the reverse of it. When taking $p=-1$, the soft-first ordering of $\kt$ turns
into a hard-first one. Effectively, it is the $\kt$ algorithm with the 
role of soft and hard interchanged, and the "anti" part shows exactly that
inversion. The two sit at opposite ends of the generalised family, with
Cambridge/Aachen, free of any transverse momentum dependence, between them.

There is a geometric consequence because of this ordering. A hard particle with
no other hard particle within an an angular distance $2R$ gathers all softer inputs
within a distance $R$ of it, producing a jet that is exactly circular in the $(y,\phi)$
plane. When two hard particles are closer than $2R$, the border between their jets
is set by their relative transverse momenta with the harder one retains the larger
share, but the jets remain regular in shape. Due to soft radiation being last in the
clustering, it cannot deform these borders. They are spread across the hard jets 
without chaging their shape.

The region of the $(y,\phi)$ plane from which a jet gathers its inputs is a useful
way to describe this stability. This area can be probed operationally by adding a 
large number of infinitesimal soft particles to an event and record which jet each of these
particles is assigned. For the majoroty of the algorithms, the resulting areas are irregular 
and fluctuate from jet to jet, because soft particles impact the clustering. However,
for the \antikt{} algorithm, where soft particles are clustered last and cannot reshape the hard jets, the
areas are close to the ideal value $\pi R^2$ and fluctuate little. A stable and expected
area is what makes the subtraction of pile-up and underlying-event contamination
tractable, since that contamination scales with the area over which each jet
collects it.

This regularity is what made the \antikt{} algorithm the standard jet
definition at the LHC. Since the jet border is stable against soft radiation,
the area over which a jet collects contamination from pile-up and the underlying
event is well defined and almost constant, and the corresponding energy
corrections are simple to calibrate. Therefore, the \antikt{} algorithm combines
the IRC safety guaranteed for the whole generalised-$\kt$ family with a jet
form that is simple to treat experimentally. This combination thus provides
regular borders, which the other two generalised-$\kt$ algorithms do not.

The importance of the \antikt{} is also a matter of history. When the LHC
started taking data, an IRC safe jet definitions was needed, so that measurements
could be compared with the increasingly precise perturbative calculations
that became available. The algorithm was also required to be fast enough to run
along the very large event samples which the LHC would produce. The \antikt{} algorithm,
together with its efficient implementation in the \textsc{FastJet}
package \cite{Cacciari_2012}, met both needs, and it was adopted as the baseline jet
definition by the LHC experiments, a role it has held since.

It is because of this that the \antikt{} algorithm was used in this work to define
the jets, with radius parameter $R = 0.8$. Jets constructed in this way are
conventionally denoted as AK8. Howver, the algorithm does not provide a physically
meaningful account of how the jet was assembled. This is becacause its sequence is
ordered by hardness rather than by angle and the intermediate recombinations bear
no correspondence to the sequence of physical splittings in a parton shower,  which is
ordered in angle. The clustering history of an \antikt{} jet is, in this sense, an
artefact of the algorithm rather than a record of the underlying radiation.
Recovering that history, which is what the substructure observables of this work
require, calls for a different member of the family, applied to the constituents
of the jet once it has been defined. That algorithm is the Cambridge/Aachen algorithm
and it is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{The Cambridge/Aachen algorithm}
\label{sec:ca}

From equation \ref{eq:dij} and equation \ref{eq:diB}, if one chooses $p=0$, the Cambridge/Aachen (C/A)
algorithm is defined \cite{Dokshitzer_1997}-\cite{wobisch1999hadronizationcorrectionsjetcross}.
With this choice, the transverse momentum factors drop out of the distance measure entirely,
leaving the equation to a purely angular form:

\begin{equation}
  d_{ij} = \frac{\dR_{ij}^2}{R^2}.
  \label{eq:ca-distance}
\end{equation}

The clustering process with this algorithm proceeds by repeatedly merging the pair of inputs
closest in angle, independently of how hard or soft they are. Where the \antikt{}
algorithm is driven by the hardest inputs, the C/A algorithm orders them by angular
separation alone. This means that the first merges are the most collinear and the 
last the widest.

This type of angluar ordering is what makes the C/A algorithm the optimal tool choice
for evaluating jet substructure. As discussed in Section \ref{sec:qcd}, the parton
shower is ordered in angle, with the widest angle splittings happening earliest
in the physical branching sequence. Therefore, the C/A algorithm mirrors, in reverse,
the order in which the shower developed. By undoing the clustering process one step at 
a time, an approximation of the branching sequence is retraced from the widest splitting
to the narrowest splitting, and each step exposes a  single emission, characterised by the
angular separation $\Delta R$ and the relative transverse momentum $k_t$ of the two branches it separates.
This agreement between the C/A history and the physical shower is what the Lund jet plane,
discussed in Section \ref{sec:lund}, is built to exploit, and it is the reason
jet substructure observables are extracted from the C/A history rather than the
\antikt{} one.

The declustering process can be asserted explicitly. Starting from the single C/A
object that contains the whole jet, one undoes the last recombination, splitting it
into two subjets $a$ and $b$, labelled so that $p_{t,a} > p_{t,b}$, meaning $a$ is
the harder branch and $b$ the softer branch.
This softer branch is taken as the emission and then, the declustering process, follows
the harder branch $a$, repeating the previous step of splitting it.
Iterating along the hard branch until it
can no longer be declustered traces out the primary sequence of the jet. This is 
a ordered list of emissions, from the widest and earliest to the most collinear
and latest.

Every splitting in this sequence is characterised by a small set of kinematic variables.
The angular separation of the two branches $\dR_{ab}$, and their relative transverse
momentum $k_t = p_{t,b}\,\dR_{ab}$, together locate the emission on the two scales
that matter. This characterises the splitting by how hard and how hard it is. A third variable,
the momentum fraction $z = p_{t,b}/(p_{t,a} + p_{t,b})$, computes how the momentum was distributed
between the emission and the emitter.
These are the quantities from which the Lund jet plane of section \ref{sec:lund} is
constructed, and computing them at every declustering step is the immediate goal of the C/A history.

By following only the harder branch, at each step, one captures the primary emissions, although
it discards the structure within the softer branches. In order to recover that structure, it is
necessary to decluster it recursively. By choosing a primary emission, the softer branch
$b$ is itself declustered in the same way, exposing the emission within it and generating a secondary
sequence of emissions associated with that primary splitting. The primary
sequence records how the leading parton radiated and the secondary sequences record how
those radiated partons radiated in turn. In this work, both primary and secondary emissions'
observables are needed in order to compare the orientation of a primary splitting with
that of the secondary splitting it seeds.

In practice, the \antikt{} and C/A algorithms are applied in succession and complement each other.
A jet is first defined with the \antikt{} algorithm, since it fixes its constituents and
its regular boundary. Those constituents are then extracted and reclustered with the C/A algorithm,
in order to obtain an angularly ordered history that can expose the emissions within a jet.
In this work, the radius parameter of the C/A reclustering step was set to $R=2$, larger than the
\antikt{} value of $R=0.8$, to ensure that all the constituents of the original jet
are gathered into a single C/A object and that the declustering history is complete
with no emission lost at the edge. Thankfully, the kinematics of the jet are unchanged
by performing this operation because the same constituents with the same momentum are 
involved, however they now become organised into a sequence from which the physical
splittings can be read.

The division of labour between the two algorithms is thus deliberate. The
\antikt{} algorithm supplies a jet with a stable, regular boundary, well suited to
the energy calibration and pile-up subtraction discussed in
Section \ref{sec:antikt}. The C/A algorithm applied to that jet's constituents
supplies a history that mirrors the physical showering sequence with angularly ordered
branching history which substructure requires. Neither algorithm alone would suffice.
The \antikt{} algorithm gives a clean jet but an unphysical history, while the
C/A algorithm gives a physical history but an irregular, contamination-prone boundary.
Using each where it is strong is what makes the substructure analysis of this work both
experimentally robust and physically interpretable.

\subsection{Flavoured jets and the IFN algorithm}
\label{sec:ifn}

In the previous subsections, the algorithms cluster inputs on the basis of their
kinematics alone, which is sufficient for most purposes. However, for the core measurement
of this work, kinematics alone are not enough. As foreseen in Chapter \ref{cap:introducao},
the goal is to explore the spin correlations of gluon splitting, and that requires
distinguishing the channels of equations \eqref{eq:Pgg}, \eqref{eq:Pgq} and \eqref{eq:Pqq},
which produce, respectively, a pair of gluons, a quark or anti quark with a gluon and a quark-antiquark pair.
The channels differ not only in their rates but also their spin interference signal carries
different signs and magnitudes. Evaluating this effect at its real strengh thus require
separating channels, and, in addition, that requires flavour assigment to the splittings
inside a jet. This raises the obstacle of how can one assign flavour to a jet, or even
to a branch inside one, in a way that remains theoretically well defined and experimentally robust.

The most obvious procedure is to sum the flavour of a jet's constituents, by counting
a quark as $+1$ and an antiquark as $-1$ for each quark flavour separatelly, so
that the net flavour of a jet is that of the parton which initiated it. Although
this procedure is simple, it is not IRC safe and the root of the problem is precisely the
$g \to q\bar{q}$ splitting. A gluon carries no net flavour, but it can split into
a quark-antiquark pair that is soft or wide in angle or even both. This quark and 
antiquark can then be assigned to different jets, or even to different branches of
the same jet, making each acquire a net flavour that the gluon never possessed.
Because the splitting can be arbitrarily soft, 
the missassigment occurs with a probability that diverges in the same manner as the
emission spectrum of equation \eqref{eq:soft-collinear}, and no virtual correction
is assigned to cancel it. This is the same mechanism for kinematic observables,
identified in Section \ref{sec:ircsafety} but now for the flavour. Here, this
simple procedure is ruined by exactly soft and collinear radiation that 
QCD produces most abundantly, and a fixed order calculation of it fails to converge.
Thus, flavour assigned sloppily is not a physical quantity and it cannot be compared with theory.

Restoring IRC safety to jet flavour has been pursued for two decades.
The first safe prescriptions achieved IRC safety by making the clustering
distance itself depend on the flavour of the objects being combined \cite{Banfi_2006},
which succeeded in supressing the divergence, but they costed the produced jets 
to not have the kinematics of the fundamental algorithm. This means that a flavour
aware jet and its equivalent \antikt{} or C/A jet were no longer the same object.
For a measurement that requires both kinematics of a jet and the flavour of its structure,
that cost is too high. In this work, the approach adopted was using the Interleaved Flavour Neutralisation (IFN)
\cite{caola2023flavouredjetsexactantikt}, which was designed exactly to avoid that issue.
IFN leaves the kinematics of the clusturing entirely untouched and treats flavour as
a separate layer that runs alongside the normal clustering sequence. The jets, which are returned
by this algorithm, are the identical to the C/A jets of Section \ref{sec:ca}. This includes 
constituents and momentums. What IFN gives in addition is a flavour label for each, computed by
a procedure that is stable against soft and collinear emissions.

The principle behind this new procedure is neutralisation. While the clustering occurs,
IFN tracks down the flavour carried by every object in the sequence, and, at each stage,
asks wether a flavoured object can be matched to a nearby object of opposite flavour,
which is the partner who would have originated the common $g \to q\bar{q}$ splitting.
Whenever it finds such a pair, the two flavours are neutralised against each other,
so that the ingenuine flavour is cancelled at the moment it would otherwise be passed
up the clustering tree and inherited by a jet. This makes the quark and antiquark born
of the same soft or collinear gluon splitting reunite in flavour, even though their kinematics
remain whatever the C/A algorithm placed them. The matching is ruled by a flavour distance
that weighs how soft and how collinear a candidate pair is, so that it is exactly the 
soft and collinear configurations that are neutralised,
while genuinely distinct hard flavours are left in place. 

What IFN brings to this work is that it does not merely give a prescription that labels
the final jet. It exposes the flavour at every step of the clustering and not only at the end.
This is the feature that the present analysis required. he clustering of a
C/A history produces at each step a splitting of one object into two branches. IFN
attaches to those branches a flavour label that is IRC safe and, therefore, meaningful
at fixed order in perturbation theory. The abstract distinction between the discussed
channels in Eqs. \eqref{eq:Pgg}, \eqref{eq:Pgq} and \eqref{eq:Pqq} is thereby made concrete, and
it can be read off at each splitting in the history of a jet. It is on this per splitting flavour
that the channel selection at the heart of the measurement is built.
The algorithm is distributed as a
plugin to the \textsc{FastJet} package \cite{Cacciari_2012}, and the configuration
adopted here, together with the way its per-splitting labels are combined into the
selection itself, is set out in Chapter \ref{cap:metodos}.

\section{The Lund jet plane}
\label{sec:lund}

Described in Section \ref{sec:ca}, the declustering of the C/A history, links
with every jet an ordered sequence of emissions, each carrying an
angular separation $\dR$, a relative transverse momentum $k_t$ and a momentum 
fraction $z$. This sequence, on its own, is just a list of numbers. Jets
have different numbers of emissions and the raw variables reach many orders of magnitude.
However, the Lund jet plane \cite{Dreyer:2018nbf} can represent this list into something
clear, when mapping each emission to a single point in a two-dimensional 
plane whose coordinates are chosen so that the structure of QCD radiation is exposed 
in its simple form.

\subsection{Coordinates and the uniform density}
\label{sec:lund-uniform}

The procedure to construct the Lund jet plane is direct. For each jet, the primary
declustering of the C/A history, of Section \ref{sec:ca}, yields a list of emissions.
Every emission in that list is mapped to a single point on the plane using its 
angular separation $\dR$ and relative transverse momentum $k_t$ as coordinates.
When repeating this for every emission in a sample and overlaying the resulting points,
it produces a two-dimensional distribution, which is then normalised to the number of jets inside
the sample, giving the average density of emission per unit area. It is this averaged distribution,
rather than the pattern of any single jet, that is referred to as the Lund jet plane
and that carries the statistical information exploited below. 

Each primary emission is placed on the plane at the coordinates:

\begin{equation}
  \left( \ln\frac{1}{\dR},\; \ln\kt \right),
  \label{eq:lund-coords}
\end{equation}

the horizontal axis measures the inverse angle of the splitting and the vertical
axis its hardness, both logarithmically. The choice of logarithmic axes is
not cosmetic. Going back to Section \ref{sec:qcd} that the emission probability
of a gluon emission carries the double structure $d\mathcal{P} \propto \alpha_s\, d\theta/\theta \; dE/E$,
which is one factor for the collinear enhancement and one for the soft enhancement.
When written in terms of Lund coordinates of equation \eqref{eq:lund-coords}, each
of these factors becomes flat. The measure $d\theta/\theta$ is simply $d\ln(1/\dR)$, and the energy factor
maps onto $d\ln\kt$. The doubly divergent emission spectrum of the raw variables
therefore becomes, on the Lund plane, an approximately uniform density of
emissions per unit area,

\begin{equation}
  \rho \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\text{jet}}}\,
  \frac{dn_{\text{emissions}}}{d\ln(1/\dR)\; d\ln\kt}
  \;\approx\; \text{const}\times\frac{\alpha_s}{\pi},
  \label{eq:lund-density}
\end{equation}

up to the slow running of $\alpha_s$ and the effects discussed below. It is this
flatness that makes the plane so useful against a uniform background. Any physical 
effect beyond the leading soft-collinear approximation therefore appears as a 
localised enhancement or suppression, rather than being obscured by the rapidly falling raw spectrum.
This uniform density is precisely what is observed in practice. Figure \ref{fig:lund-example}
shows the average primary Lund plane obtained from a simulated sample of QCD jets.
Through the perturbative majority of the plane the density is approximately constant,
as anticipated by equation \eqref{eq:lund-density}, falling away only towards the
boundaries of the kinematically accessible region.

\subsection{Regions of the plane}
\label{sec:lund-regions}

The reachable area of the plane is delimited, and its interior can be divided into
different regions with distinct physical source, sketched in
Figure \ref{fig:lund-schematic}. The upper boundary is set by the kinematics of
the jet. Every emission cannot be harder than the jet which contains it, so it
caps $\ln\kt$ from above. Along the right of the plane, at large $\ln(1/\dR)$, lie the collinear emissions,
those radiated at small angle to their emitter. Towards the bottom, at small
$\ln\kt$, lie the soft emissions. The perturbative description of
Section \ref{sec:qcd} holds across the majority of this region, where $\kt$ is large
enough for $\alpha_s$ to remain small, making the density there be the near-constant 
value of equation \eqref{eq:lund-density}.

The exceptional region in the lower-right corner, at both small angle and low transverse
momentum. In that region, $\kt$ falls to the scale of a few hundred~MeV, $\alpha_s$ grows
large, and the perturbative description breaks down. This region is known as the 
non-perturbative region and it is populated by hadronisation and by the soft, wide-angle
contamination present inside the event. This way, one strong feature of the Lund jet plane
is that these non-perturbative effects are confined to a restricted corner of the plane,
clearly separated from the perturbative part and not mixed with the whole observable.
A measurement can therefofore restrict, by eye or by an specific cut, to the region where
theory is applicable.

\subsection{Grooming and the primary and secondary planes}
\label{sec:lund-grooming}

This clear separation motivates grooming methods. Grooming is the intentional removal of soft,
wide-angle emissions that dominate non-perturbative regions of the Lund jet plane.
The grooming method used in this work is Soft Drop \cite{Larkoski_2014}, which
walks through the declustering and, at each step, keeps the harder branch unless
the softer one satisfies the following:

\begin{equation}
  z \;>\; z_{\text{cut}} \left( \frac{\dR}{R} \right)^{\beta},
  \label{eq:softdrop}
\end{equation}

in which case both branches are kept and the declustering precedure continues. The parameter
$z_{\text{cut}}$ specifies how soft an emission must be to be discarded, and $\beta$
manages the angular reach of the grooming. The choice $\beta = 0$, adopted here for
the Soft Drop in this work, makes the condition purely one of momentum fraction , $z > z_{\text{cut}}$, independent of angle.
In this condition, Soft Drop becomes a modified Mass-Drop Tagger \cite{Dasgupta_2013}.
Applying this grooming method becomes very visible on the Lund jet plane because it removes
the emissions in the soft, low $\kt$ band, clearing the non-perturbative corner and 
leaving a plane populated only by the harder splittings.

In the present work, two versions of the Lund jet plane are built and used as follows. The primary
Lund plane is built with the emissions along the hard branch of the jet and describes how the leading parton radiated.
The secondary Lund plane is built by taking the chosen primary emission and declustering its softer branch in turn,
using the same method as the primary Lund plane, as described in Section \ref{sec:ca}. The resulting 
points describe how that radiated parton itself went on to radiate. The two planes together deliver
complementary information and both are needed for the spin observables of Section \ref{sec:spin}, which compare the orientation of a
primary splitting with that of the secondary splitting it seeds. Figure~\ref{fig:lund-figure} illustrates these two levels of structure
for two example jets. The middle row shows the full Lund diagram, in which every
emission, primary and secondary, is represented, while the bottom row shows
the primary Lund plane alone, retaining a single point for each primary splitting.

  \cite{Dreyer:2018nbf}.}

\section{Collinear spin correlations}
\label{sec:spin}

In the previous sections, the tools assembled describe a parton shower as a sequence of
independent splittings, each blind to those before it. This description is incomplete since
intermediate parton in a shower carry spin and their spin is correlated with the kinematics 
of the splitting that produces them. Furthermore, when that parton branches, that correlation
is transmitted to the azimuthal orientation of its own decay products. So, successive
splittings are therefore not independent, meaning that the plane of one branching is 
correlated with the plane of the next. These are the collinear spin correlations which this work
presents, and they are the subject of this section.

This correlation effect is a quantum interference phenomenon, with no counterpart in a
classical picture of sequential radiation, and it shows in a distinctive and robust way.
This phenomenon manifests as a modulation of the azimuthal angle between 
successive splitting planes with a $\pi$ periodicity instead of $2\pi$, 
varying directly as a function of $\cos 2\psi$. This section develops that statement
in four steps. Section~\ref{sec:spin-mechanism} shows the physical mechanism and the origin
of the $\cos 2\psi$ modulation. Section~\ref{sec:spin-channel} shows how the size
and sign of the modulation depend on the splitting channel, the property that
makes flavour identification essential to the measurement. 
Sections~\ref{sec:spin-dpsi} and~\ref{sec:spin-eeec} then introduce the two
observables through which this work accesses the effect.
These observables being the Lund declustering angle $\dpsi$, computed from 
the branching history of Section \ref{sec:lund}, and the three-point energy correlator, which reaches the same physics through the flow of
energy within the jet.

\subsection{Mechanism and the \texorpdfstring{$\cos 2\psi$}{cos 2psi} modulation}
\label{sec:spin-mechanism}

The origin of the correlation lies in the spin of the intermediate gluon \cite{Karlberg_2021}. 
Consider a gluon produced in a first splitting that subsequently undergoes a second splitting 
of its own. This corresponds to the setup of the primary and secondary Lund planes discussed 
in Section \ref{sec:lund}. Since the gluon is a spin-one particle, a hard, nearly on-shell 
gluon possesses only two physical polarisation states, both transverse to its direction of 
motion. In the collinear limit, these states are most naturally described not as circular 
polarisations, but as two orthogonal linear polarisations, each representing an oscillation 
of the field along a fixed axis in the plane transverse to the gluon momentum.

The polarisation of the intermediate gluon is not random. The first splitting,
which produced it, selects a preferred transverse axis. Therefore, the gluon emerges linearly
polarized with its polarisation correlated with the plane of that first branching.
This is the main and essential point. A gluon radiated in a splitting carries a memory
of the orientation of that splitting, engraved in the direction of its linear polarisation.

Afterward, that memory is read out by the second splitting. The probability that a linearly
polarised glluon branches into a given final state depends on the azimuthal orientation
of the second splitting plane in respect with the polarisation axis. Writing $\psi$ for the angle between the first
and the second splitting planes, the differential rate acquires a dependence on $\psi$ through
this alignment, and the two splittings become correlated in their relative azimuth.

The period of that correlation is fixed by the nature of the polarisation. A
linear polarisation is specified by an axis, not by a direction along it. Rotating
the polarisation by $\pi$ returns the same physical state, since an oscillation
along an axis is unchanged by reversing its sense. The azimuthal dependence must
therefore be invariant under $\psi \to \psi + \pi$, which forbids a $\cos\psi$ term
and admits $\cos 2\psi$ as its leading harmonic. This is the distinctive signature of the effect.
The result has to have a modulation of period $\pi$ rather than $2\pi$, reflecting the
headless, axial character of linear polarisation. Collinear spin correlations
announce themselves not as any azimuthal dependence, but specifically as a
$\cos 2\psi$ dependence.

At second order in the strong coupling and in the collinear limit, the cross
section differential in $\psi$ therefore takes the form

\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\sigma}{d\psi} \;\propto\; a_0 \left( 1 + \frac{a_2}{a_0}\cos 2\psi \right),
  \label{eq:cos2psi}
\end{equation}

where $a_0$ is the spin-averaged rate, or, in other words, the result one would obtain by ignoring
the polarisation of the intermediate gluon, and $a_2$ is the coefficient of the
interference term \cite{Karlberg_2021}. The ratio $a_2/a_0$ measures the strength of the correlation where 
it vanishes if the intermediate gluon carries no polarisation memory, and its
magnitude sets the depth of the $\cos 2\psi$ modulation. Its sign determines the
phase, wether the second splitting preferentially aligns with the first
splitting plane, giving maxima at $\psi = 0, \pi$, or preferentially lies
perpendicular to it, giving maxima at $\psi = \pm\pi/2$. The measurement of these
spin correlations reduces, in essence, to the measurement of this single ratio,
and the whole of the analysis that follows is directed towards extracting it
cleanly.

\subsection{Channel dependence of \texorpdfstring{$a_2/a_0$}{a2/a0}}
\label{sec:spin-channel}

The process described in Section \ref{sec:spin-mechanism} was made for a generic
intermediate gluon, however, the strength and sign of the resulting modulation are not
universal. These parameters depend on the nature of the second splitting, or, in other words,
on the channel through which the polarised gluon decays. The two channels available are those
of equations \eqref{eq:Pgg} and \eqref{eq:Pgq}. The gluon can spit into a pair
of gluons, $g \to gg$, or into a quark-antiquark pair, $g \to q\bar{q}$. Each of these
channels read out the polarisation of the parent gluon, but each one of them do so with opposite
sign and different strengh \cite{Karlberg_2021}.

The reason behind this difference comes from the spin of the produced particles. The angular
The angular distribution of a splitting depends on how the daughters' spins couple to the
polarisation axis of the parent, and this coupling differs between a final state
of two vector gluons and one of two spin-half fermions \cite{Karlberg_2021}. The
difference is that the interference coefficient $a_2/a_0$ of equation \eqref{eq:cos2psi}
appears with one sign for the $g \to gg$ channel and the opposite sign for the $g \to q\bar{q}$ channel,
where one channel prefers the alignment of the two splitting planes, the other a perpendicular
configuration.

This sign difference is the main goal which this work is built to overcome.
If one does a measurement without distinguishing the two channels of the 
second splitting, the contributions of both sum together, leaving a result
which almost cancels itself. The endured signal measured in this case is not
the intrinsic strength of the correlation but a residue, because its magnitude
and even its sign are set by the balance between the $g \to gg$ and $g \to q\bar{q}$ 
channels present in the sample. However, this interference is not absent. It is just
diluted and this dilution reflects the flavour composition of the selection rather than any
property of QCD.

Thus, it is required to isolate a single channel in order to make a reliable measurement
of the effect. This work shows the selection procedure of the $g \to q\bar{q}$ splittings,
and measures the parameter $a_2/a_0$ on that subsample alone, replacing the diluted inclusive
residue with the coefficient of a single channel. This is precisely what the infrared-safe and
collinear-safe flavour labelling of Section~\ref{sec:ifn} makes possible. IFN tags
the flavour, exposed during the declustering, of each splitting, allowing the
$g \to q\bar{q}$ channel to be enriched and the $\cos 2\psi$ modulation to be
measured as a function of the purity of that enrichment. The channel dependence
described here is therefore not just a feature of the physics but the reason the
flavour selection developed in this work is required, and it is the procedure
that connects the spin observable of the next section to the flavoured-jet
algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:ifn}.

\subsection{The Lund observable \texorpdfstring{$\dpsi$}{Delta psi 12}}
\label{sec:spin-dpsi}

The effect described in the previous sections can be studied using a concrete observable,
given by the Lund declustering of Section \ref{sec:lund}, in order to indentify
the two splittings whose planes are to be compared. This construction was proposed in
\cite{Karlberg_2021}, and the observable is the azimuthal angle 
$\dpsi$ between the hardest primary and secondary branchings of a jet.

The two splittings are selected as it follows. When declustering the primary Lund
sequence of a jet, one selects the splitting which passes the Soft Drop condition
of equation \eqref{eq:softdrop} and has the largest relative transverse momentum
$\kt$ among all the primary sequence. This splitting is then called the primary splitting. 
Then, declustering the soft branch of this selected
primary splitting, we build the secondary sequence generated by it. Thus, with the same
procedure as in the primary sequence, one selects the splitting which passes the Soft Drop
condition of equation \eqref{eq:softdrop} and has the largest relative transverse momentum
$\kt$ among all the secondary sequence. This splitting is called the secondary splitting.
Each of the two chosen splittings defines a plane in momentum space. 
The plane $\mathcal{P}_1$ spanned by the branches of the first,
and $\mathcal{P}_2$ spanned by the branches of the second. With this, $\dpsi$ is the
azimuthal angle between them, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:psi-definition}.

By construction, $\dpsi$ is the realisation for jets of the abstract angle $\psi$
of equation \eqref{eq:cos2psi}. Its distribution therefore inherits the $\cos 2\psi$
modulation derived in Section~\ref{sec:spin-mechanism},

\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{d\sigma}{d\dpsi} \;\propto\;
  1 + \frac{a_2}{a_0}\cos 2\dpsi,
  \label{eq:dpsi-modulation}
\end{equation}

and the coefficient $a_2/a_0$ extracted from it is the quantity that carries the
spin-correlation physics. Two features of the definition are essential to what
follows. First, because the second splitting is drawn from the secondary sequence
seeded by the first, $\dpsi$ compares precisely the two successive branchings
between which the polarisation memory of Section~\ref{sec:spin-mechanism} is
transmitted. Second, when that second splitting is the decay of an intermediate
gluon, its channel $g \to gg$ or $g \to q\bar{q}$ can be identified by the flavour
labelling of Section~\ref{sec:ifn}. Splittings of the form $q \to qg$, in which
no intermediate gluon is present, carry no correlation and act only as a
background to be removed. It is this flavour resolution that makes $\dpsi$ not
merely a measurement of the inclusive, diluted modulation, but a channel-resolved
probe of the effect. By restricting to secondary splittings tagged as
$g \to q\bar{q}$, and thereby separating them both from $g \to gg$ splittings and
from the correlation-free $q \to qg$ background, the sign cancellation of
Section~\ref{sec:spin-channel} is undone, and $a_2/a_0$ can be measured on a
single channel.

\subsection{The three-point energy correlator}
\label{sec:spin-eeec}

Although the lund observable described in the previous section gives a result for the
collinear spin correlations of Section \ref{sec:spin-mechanism}, it is not the route
to do so. The $\cos 2\psi$ modulation can be accessed through the energy flow within a jet
and without any reference to any clustering history, using the three-point energy correlator
(EEEC) \Cite{Chen_2021}. Where $\dpsi$ reconstructs the two splitting planes by
declustering, the energy correlator reads the effect directly from the angular and
energy distribution of the jet's constituents, and the two therefore provide
complementary handles on the same underlying physics.

The EEEC measures the energy-weighted distribution of
triplets of particles as a function of their relative angles. Concretely, it is
defined as the energy-weighted sum over all triplets of particles in the jet,

\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{d^3\Sigma}{d\psi\, d\theta_S\, d\theta_L}
  = \sum_{i,j,k}
  \frac{\pt{}_{,i}\,\pt{}_{,j}\,\pt{}_{,k}}{\pt{}_{,\text{jet}}^{\,3}}\,
  \delta(\psi - \psi_{ijk})\,
  \delta(\theta_S - \theta_{S,ijk})\,
  \delta(\theta_L - \theta_{L,ijk}),
  \label{eq:eeec}
\end{equation}

where the sum runs over all triplets $(i,j,k)$ of the jet's constituents, each
weighted by the product of their transverse momenta normalised to that of the
jet. Here $\theta_S$ and $\theta_L$ are the smallest and largest opening angles
of the triplet, and $\psi$ is the azimuthal angle between the plane spanned by
the two closest particles and that spanned by the third. Its power for the present
purpose is that this last variable retains sensitivity to the azimuthal
orientation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:spin-mechanism}. This means that the
correlator's dependence on $\psi$ carries the same $\cos 2\psi$ modulation, governed by the
same interference between gluon polarisation states. Being built from energy
weights and angles alone, the correlator is infrared and collinear safe by
construction and requires no declustering, no grooming and no jet-flavour
assigment.

This makes the energy correlator an independent cross-check on the physics
extracted through $\dpsi$. It reaches the same $\cos 2\psi$ effect by a route that
shares none of the reconstruction choices on which the Lund observable depends,
so that agreement between the two would establish that the measured modulation is a
property of the radiation rather than an artefact of any one analysis chain. 
Note that the coefficient $a_2/a_0$ extracted from the energy correlator is not expected to
coincide numerically with that from $\dpsi$, since the two observables define the
azimuthal angle through different objects and carry different sensitivities to the
kinematic selection. What the two share is the sign and the qualitative behaviour
dictated by the underlying gluon-polarisation interference, and it is at that
level that they cross-check one another.
The correlator is developed in this work as a complementary probe alongside the primary
$\dpsi$ measurement, and its implementation and results are presented in
Chapter~\ref{cap:resultados}.
